Probability spaces, conditional probability and random variables

“Count the favourable outcomes and divide” only works when every outcome is equally likely. Here we build the general machinery (a sample space, an event space and a probability function), learn how new information changes a probability, see what independence really means, and turn outcomes into numbers we can average. Neutron transport theory is written in exactly this language.

≈ 80 min read9 animationsSections 19–34 of the Class 2 notes

By the end you will be able to

  • describe any random experiment as a probability space $(\Omega, \sigma(\Omega), \Pr)$ and say what each of the three parts does;
  • check whether a collection of subsets is an event space (a σ-algebra), and build the smallest one that contains a given event;
  • use the axioms of probability to derive $\Pr(\emptyset) = 0$, the complement rule and the rule for the probability of a union;
  • compute a conditional probability by treating the given event as the new sample space, and use the multiplication rule;
  • test two events for independence, and explain why mutually exclusive events with non-zero probabilities are always dependent;
  • define a random variable as a measurable function, find preimages, and write down its probability distribution;
  • compute the mean, variance, standard deviation and moments of a discrete random variable.

Before we startFrom counting to a framework

In Class 1 (Sections 1 and 7) we computed a probability as a ratio of counts,

$$\Pr(E) = \frac{n(E)}{n(\Omega)}.$$

That rule quietly assumes two things: that the experiment has a finite set of outcomes, and that all outcomes are equally likely. A fair die satisfies both. A neutron does not. The distance a neutron travels before it collides can be any positive number, so there are infinitely many outcomes, and a neutron is far more likely to collide after a short flight than after a long one.

In this class we build the general framework, called probability theory, which works without those two restrictions. Then we use it to understand conditional probability, independence and random variables. Take it slowly: each idea is first explained in words, then stated formally, then tried on an example.

Chapter 3 The probability space Three objects that together describe any random experiment.

Section 19What is probability theory?

Think of any random experiment: tossing a coin, rolling a die, or following one neutron from the moment it is born in a fission until it is absorbed or escapes from the reactor. Before we can say anything precise about it, we have to settle three things.

  • What can happen? The list of all possible outcomes.
  • What are we allowed to ask about? The yes/no questions (“did the neutron leak out?”, “did at least one coin show Head?”) that we want to attach probabilities to.
  • How likely is each answer? A number between 0 and 1 for each such question.

Probability theory turns these three things into three mathematical objects.

Definition · probability space

Probability theory is the mathematical study of random experiments. It describes a random experiment with three objects:

  1. the sample space $\Omega$: the set of all outcomes;
  2. the event space $\sigma(\Omega)$: the collection of subsets of $\Omega$ to which we will assign a probability;
  3. the probability function $\Pr$: a rule that assigns a number to every event in $\sigma(\Omega)$.

The triple $(\Omega, \sigma(\Omega), \Pr)$ is called a probability space.

Every question in probability is a question about some probability space, whether or not it is written down explicitly. When a textbook says “a fair die is rolled”, it silently means: $\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$, every subset of $\Omega$ is an event, and each face has probability $\tfrac16$. The next three sections define each object in turn.

Key idea

$\Omega$ says what can happen. $\sigma(\Omega)$ says which questions we may ask. $\Pr$ answers those questions with numbers.

In real life · neutron transport

Monte Carlo transport codes used in reactor physics (OpenMC and MCNP are well-known examples) simulate neutrons one at a time. The simulated life of one neutron, from birth until it is absorbed or escapes, is called a history. In the language of this class, one history is one outcome $\omega$, and the set of all possible histories is $\Omega$. A question such as “was this neutron absorbed in the fuel?” is an event. The code estimates its probability by simulating very many histories and counting how often the event occurs.

Check yourself

Write down the three parts of a probability space for one roll of a fair die.

$\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$. For the event space we can take every subset of $\Omega$, i.e. the power set $2^{\Omega}$, which has $2^6 = 64$ members. The probability function is $\Pr(E) = n(E)/6$; for example $\Pr(\{2, 4, 6\}) = 3/6 = 1/2$.

Section 20Sample space

Before asking “how likely?”, we list what can happen. Each item on the list is one complete, detailed result of the experiment, called an outcome. Exactly one outcome occurs every time we perform the experiment.

Definition · sample space

The set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment is called the sample space. We denote it by $\Omega$ (Greek capital “omega”). Each outcome is an element of $\Omega$.

  • Toss one coin: $\Omega = \{H, T\}$.
  • Toss two coins: $\Omega = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$.
  • Roll one die: $\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$.
  • Count the tosses needed to see the first Head: $\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$, an infinite set.

Two small remarks help later.

  • Outcomes never overlap. Each time the experiment is run, exactly one element of $\Omega$ happens. “HT” and “TH” are different outcomes: in the first the first coin is the Head, in the second the second one is.
  • The sample space depends on what we record. If we only wrote down the number of Heads, we could use $\{0, 1, 2\}$. That is a legitimate sample space, but its three outcomes are not equally likely, so the finer list $\{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$ is more convenient. We will return to this idea when we meet random variables in Section 29.

Infinite sample spaces come in two sizes. $\{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$ can be listed one element after another; it is countably infinite. The set of all distances a neutron might travel before its first collision is the interval $(0, \infty)$. That set cannot be listed even in principle; it is uncountable. Uncountable sample spaces are the reason we need the next section.

In real life · one neutron, three sample spaces

Ask “what finally happens to this neutron?” and a coarse sample space is $\Omega = \{$leaks out, absorbed without fission, causes fission$\}$. Ask “how many times does it scatter before it is absorbed or leaks out?” and $\Omega = \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$, infinite like the coin example. Ask “how far does it fly before its first collision?” and $\Omega = (0, \infty)$. The same physical neutron gives three different sample spaces: the sample space depends on the question whose answer we record.

(a) Write the sample space for three tosses of a coin. How many outcomes are there? (b) Is the sample space for “the time until a detector next clicks” finite, countably infinite or uncountable?

(a) $\Omega = \{HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT,$ $THH, THT, TTH, TTT\}$, so $n(\Omega) = 2^3 = 8$. (b) The time can be any positive real number, so $\Omega = (0, \infty)$, which is uncountable.

Section 21Event space: the σ-algebra

We rarely care about one exact outcome. We usually care about a description of outcomes, such as “at least one Head”. A description picks out a group of outcomes, that is, a subset of $\Omega$.

Definition · event

An event is a subset of $\Omega$, for example “at least one Head” $= \{HH, HT, TH\}$. We say the event occurs if the outcome of the experiment is one of its elements.

We do not always need to assign a probability to every subset of $\Omega$, and for some infinite sample spaces we are not even able to. (For the interval $[0, 1]$ it can be proved that there is no way to give every subset a “length” that obeys the rules of the next sections and agrees with ordinary length.) Instead we pick a set of subsets of $\Omega$, called an event space. We will denote the event space by $\sigma(\Omega)$.

If $E$ is an event in the event space, the set relationships are as follows.

Why we cannot pick just any collection

When we ask probabilistic questions, we often want the probability of combinations of events. In a two-coin experiment: what is the probability of getting at least one Head? What is the probability of getting no Head at all? These two questions belong together: the second is “not” the first. Or, in the same experiment: what is the probability that the first coin shows Head? That the second coin shows Head? That the first or the second shows Head? The third question is built from the first two with “or”.

So we cannot pick any collection we like. If we can talk about the probability of some events, then logic forces us to talk about some others too. These requirements impose the following properties on the event space.

Definition · event space (σ-algebra)
  1. $\sigma(\Omega)$ contains the sample space: $\ \Omega \in \sigma(\Omega)$.
  2. If an event $E \in \sigma(\Omega)$, then $E^{c} = \Omega \setminus E \in \sigma(\Omega)$: the event space is closed under complementation.
  3. For any number of events $E_1, E_2, \dots \in \sigma(\Omega)$, their union $E_1 \cup E_2 \cup \dots \in \sigma(\Omega)$: the event space is closed under countable unions.

A collection of subsets with these three properties is called a σ-algebra (“sigma-algebra”) on $\Omega$.

Let us understand each rule with the two-coin toss, $\Omega = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$.

(i) The sample space is an event

$\Omega$ is the event “something happens”. It always occurs, since every outcome lies in $\Omega$. It is called the sure event. Any sensible event space must contain it.

(ii) Closed under complementation

If we can ask “did $E$ occur?”, then we have automatically also asked “did $E$ not occur?”. So if $E$ is an event, $E^{c}$ must be an event.

Example. If “at least one Head” $= \{HH, HT, TH\}$ is in $\sigma(\Omega)$, then its complement “no Head” $= \{TT\}$ must also be in $\sigma(\Omega)$.

Combining (i) and (ii): the null set $\emptyset = \Omega^{c}$ is also an element of the event space. The null set is called the impossible event: no outcome lies in it, so it never occurs.

(iii) Closed under countable unions

If $E_1$ and $E_2$ are events, “$E_1$ or $E_2$ occurred” must also be an event, i.e. $E_1 \cup E_2 \in \sigma(\Omega)$. Repeating this, any finite union $E_1 \cup E_2 \cup \dots \cup E_n$ of events is an event. The rule asks for a little more: even a countably infinite union $E_1 \cup E_2 \cup \dots$ must be an event.

Example. If “both Heads” $= \{HH\}$ and “both Tails” $= \{TT\}$ are in $\sigma(\Omega)$, then “both coins show the same face” $= \{HH, TT\}$ must be in $\sigma(\Omega)$.

When $\Omega$ is finite, $\sigma(\Omega)$ is finite too, so any infinite union only repeats finitely many distinct sets, and closure under finite unions is enough. The countable version matters for infinite sample spaces such as $\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$. For example, “the first Head comes on an even toss” is an infinite union:

Using De Morgan’s laws (Class 1, Section 13) one can show that event spaces are also closed under intersections and set differences. Here is the short argument:

$$E_1 \cap E_2 = \big(E_1^{c} \cup E_2^{c}\big)^{c}, \qquad E_1 \setminus E_2 = E_1 \cap E_2^{c}.$$

Read the first identity from the inside out. $E_1^{c}$ and $E_2^{c}$ are events by rule (ii); their union is an event by rule (iii); its complement is an event by rule (ii) again. The second identity is then the intersection of two events, so it is an event as well. In everyday words: if you may ask “not” and “or”, you may also ask “and” and “but not”.

In real life · a control-room display

A plant computer reports, for each of two cooling pumps, whether it is running. If operators can ask “is pump A running?”, they can automatically ask “is pump A stopped?” (complement). With the pump B question they can also ask “is at least one pump running?” (union) and “are both running?” (intersection). The three rules of an event space are a precise version of this common sense: a sensible list of questions is closed under “not” and “or”, and therefore also under “and”.

Check yourself

For two coin tosses a friend proposes the event space $\{\emptyset, \{HH\}, \Omega\}$. Which rule fails, and how would you repair it?

Rule (ii) fails: $\{HH\}$ is in the collection but its complement $\{HT, TH, TT\}$ is not. Adding it gives $\{\emptyset, \{HH\}, \{HT, TH, TT\}, \Omega\}$, which satisfies all three rules. You will meet exactly this collection in the next section.

The power set $2^{\Omega}$ is itself a σ-algebra. Check: it contains every subset, so it certainly contains $\Omega$, all complements and all unions. It is the largest possible event space on $\Omega$.

The smallest event space containing $\{HH\}$. Suppose we only care about the event “both Heads”. What is the smallest σ-algebra $\sigma(\Omega)$ with $\{HH\} \in \sigma(\Omega)$? We add sets only when a rule forces us to.

Worked example · build σ(Ω) from {HH}

Find the smallest event space on $\Omega = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$ that contains $\{HH\}$.

Step 1. Start with what we want: $\{HH\}$.

Step 2. Rule (i): add $\Omega = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$.

Step 3. Rule (ii): add the complements. $\{HH\}^{c} = \{HT, TH, TT\}$ and $\Omega^{c} = \emptyset$.

Step 4. Rule (iii): check all unions of what we have. $\{HH\} \cup \{HT, TH, TT\} = \Omega$, and any union with $\emptyset$ or $\Omega$ gives back a set already present. Nothing new.

Step 5. Check complements again: the complement of every set in the list is already in the list. Nothing new, so we stop.

$$\boxed{\,\sigma_{HH}(\Omega) = \big\{\, \emptyset,\ \{HH\},\ \{HT, TH, TT\},\ \Omega \,\big\}\,}$$

This event space has only 4 of the 16 subsets. It can answer exactly one question, “did both coins show Head, or not?”. It cannot, for example, talk about “first coin shows Head” $= \{HH, HT\}$, because that set is not in $\sigma_{HH}(\Omega)$.

Key idea

An event space is a list of yes/no questions that is closed under “not” and “or”. A small event space means coarse information; the power set means we can answer every question about the outcome.

In real life · a coincidence counter

Two detectors look at the same source. In one short time window each detector either fires (write H) or stays silent (write T), so the outcomes are $HH, HT, TH, TT$: the same sample space as two coins. A coincidence unit gives a single output, “both fired” or “not both”. The questions its output can answer form exactly $\sigma_{HH}(\Omega) = \{\emptyset, \{HH\}, \{HT, TH, TT\}, \Omega\}$. It cannot tell you whether detector 1 fired, because $\{HH, HT\}$ is not in that event space. To answer that you must also record each detector’s own signal, and then the event space grows to all 16 subsets. An event space describes how much information a measurement gives.

(a) Find the smallest event space containing “first coin Head” $= \{HH, HT\}$. (b) How many sets are in the smallest event space containing both $\{HH\}$ and $\{HT\}$?

Section 23Probability function

A helpful picture: imagine one kilogram of sand spread over the sample space. The probability of an event is how much sand lies on it. The total amount is 1, and if you split an event into pieces that do not overlap, the sand on the pieces adds up to the sand on the whole. That is all the axioms say.

Definition · axioms of probability

Given a sample space $\Omega$ and an event space $\sigma(\Omega)$, the probability $\Pr$ is a function $\Pr : \sigma(\Omega) \to [0, 1]$ that satisfies the following properties (the axioms of probability):

  • (P1) The probability of the sample space is 1: $\ \Pr(\Omega) = 1$.
  • (P2) For mutually exclusive events $E_1, E_2, \dots \in \sigma(\Omega)$, the probability of their union is the sum of the individual probabilities: $$\Pr(E_1 \cup E_2 \cup \dots) = \Pr(E_1) + \Pr(E_2) + \dots$$

The triple $(\Omega, \sigma(\Omega), \Pr)$ is called a probability space.

Notice that the values of $\Pr$ lie in $[0, 1]$, so a probability is never negative. That requirement is built into the codomain of the function. Notice also that $\Pr$ is defined on events (sets), not on individual outcomes. When we write $\Pr(\{HH\})$ we mean the probability of the one-element event $\{HH\}$.

23.1 Some important properties

Everything else about probability is derived from (P1) and (P2). Here are the first three consequences.

  • Probability of the impossible event. $\Omega = \Omega \cup \emptyset$ is a disjoint union, so by (P2) $\Pr(\Omega) = \Pr(\Omega) + \Pr(\emptyset)$, i.e. $1 = 1 + \Pr(\emptyset)$. Hence $$\Pr(\emptyset) = 0.$$
  • Monotonicity of probability. If $E_1 \subseteq E_2$ then $\Pr(E_1) \le \Pr(E_2)$. Why: $E_2 = E_1 \cup (E_2 \setminus E_1)$ is a disjoint union, so by (P2) $$\Pr(E_2) = \Pr(E_1) + \Pr(E_2 \setminus E_1) \ \ge\ \Pr(E_1),$$ because $\Pr(E_2 \setminus E_1) \ge 0$. A bigger event can never be less likely.
  • Complement rule. $E$ and $E^{c}$ are mutually exclusive and $E \cup E^{c} = \Omega$. By (P2) and (P1), $\Pr(E) + \Pr(E^{c}) = 1$, so $$\Pr(E^{c}) = 1 - \Pr(E).$$
The Class 1 ratio is a special case

(Class 1, Sections 1 and 7.) On a finite $\Omega$ with all outcomes equally likely, $\Pr(E) = n(E)/n(\Omega)$. Dividing by $n(\Omega)$ is exactly what makes $\Pr(\Omega) = 1$. The axioms, however, also allow biased experiments and experiments with infinitely many outcomes.

In real life · what happens at a collision

Given that a neutron collides at some point in a material, the probability that the collision is of type $x$ (elastic scattering, radiative capture, fission, …) is $\Sigma_x / \Sigma_t$, the ratio of that reaction’s macroscopic cross-section to the total. Because the partial cross-sections add up to the total, $\Sigma_t = \Sigma_s + \Sigma_a$ (scattering plus absorption, each itself a sum of reactions), these probabilities add up to exactly 1. That is axioms (P1) and (P2) at work inside every transport code.

Check yourself

(a) The forecast says the probability of rain tomorrow is 0.3. What is the probability of no rain, and which property did you use? (b) Can a probability function on two coin tosses have $\Pr(\{HH\}) = 0.5$, $\Pr(\{HT\}) = 0.3$, $\Pr(\{TH\}) = 0.3$, $\Pr(\{TT\}) = 0$?

(a) $1 - 0.3 = 0.7$, by the complement rule. (b) No. The four simple events are mutually exclusive and their union is $\Omega$, so by (P2) and (P1) their probabilities must add to 1. Here they add to $1.1$.

Section 24Simple and composite events

Some events are as small as an event can be (a single outcome); the rest are built by joining these smallest pieces. The probability axioms make the pieces add.

Definition · simple and composite events

Simple (elementary) event: an event containing exactly one outcome, e.g. $\{HT\}$.

Composite (compound) event: an event containing more than one outcome. A composite event is a union of simple events, e.g. “at least one Head” $= \{HH\} \cup \{HT\} \cup \{TH\}$.

Worked example · two tosses of an unbiased coin

Take $\sigma(\Omega) = 2^{\Omega}$. “Unbiased” means all four simple events are equally likely. Find their probabilities, and then the probabilities of some composite events.

Step 1. The four simple events $\{HH\}, \{HT\}, \{TH\}, \{TT\}$ are mutually exclusive (no outcome is in two of them), and their union is $\Omega$.

Step 2. So axioms (P1) and (P2) give $$\Pr(\{HH\}) + \Pr(\{HT\}) + \Pr(\{TH\}) + \Pr(\{TT\}) = \Pr(\Omega) = 1.$$

Step 3. The four numbers are equal and add to 1, so each simple event has probability $\tfrac14$.

Step 4. The probability of a composite event is the sum over its simple events, by (P2):

EventSubsetTypeProbability
both Heads$\{HH\}$simple$1/4$
no Head$\{TT\}$simple$1/4$
first coin Head$\{HH, HT\}$composite$1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2$
exactly one Head$\{HT, TH\}$composite$1/2$
at least one Head$\{HH, HT, TH\}$composite$3/4$

Step 5 (check). $\Pr(\text{at least one Head}) = 1 - \Pr(\text{no Head}) = 1 - \tfrac14 = \tfrac34$, by the complement rule. The two routes agree.

A tree diagram shows the same experiment as two stages. Each path from the root to a leaf is one simple event, and every leaf gets probability $\tfrac12 \times \tfrac12 = \tfrac14$. (Why we may multiply along a path is the subject of Sections 26 and 27.)

In real life · tallies in a transport code

At each collision a Monte Carlo code samples one simple outcome, for example which reaction happens: elastic scattering, inelastic scattering, radiative capture, fission, and so on. A tally such as “the neutron was absorbed” is a composite event. It collects several simple outcomes (capture, fission and any other absorption reactions), and its probability is the sum of theirs, exactly as in the table above.

Check yourself

A fair coin is tossed three times. What is the probability of exactly two Heads?

There are 8 equally likely simple events, each with probability $\tfrac18$. “Exactly two Heads” $= \{HHT, HTH, THH\}$ is a composite event with three simple events, so its probability is $\tfrac38$.

Section 25Probability of a union

If two events overlap, simply adding their probabilities counts the overlap twice. Subtracting the overlap once puts things right. We show

$$\boxed{\,\Pr(E_1 \cup E_2) = \Pr(E_1) + \Pr(E_2) - \Pr(E_1 \cap E_2)\,}$$

For finitely many, equally likely outcomes this is easy. From Class 1, Section 11, $n(E_1 \cup E_2) = n(E_1) + n(E_2) - n(E_1 \cap E_2)$. Divide both sides by $n(\Omega)$:

$$\frac{n(E_1 \cup E_2)}{n(\Omega)} = \frac{n(E_1)}{n(\Omega)} + \frac{n(E_2)}{n(\Omega)} - \frac{n(E_1 \cap E_2)}{n(\Omega)}$$ $$\implies \Pr(E_1 \cup E_2) = \Pr(E_1) + \Pr(E_2) - \Pr(E_1 \cap E_2).$$

In general the result follows from the probability axioms. The same three-piece split works, with $\Pr$ in place of $n$, because the only thing we used about $n$ was that it adds over mutually exclusive sets.

Proof · from the axioms, in three steps

Show that $\Pr(E_1 \cup E_2) = \Pr(E_1) + \Pr(E_2) - \Pr(E_1 \cap E_2)$ for any two events.

Step 1. $E_1 \cup E_2 = (E_1 \setminus E_2) \cup (E_1 \cap E_2) \cup (E_2 \setminus E_1)$, three mutually exclusive events. By (P2): $$\Pr(E_1 \cup E_2) = \Pr(E_1 \setminus E_2) + \Pr(E_1 \cap E_2) + \Pr(E_2 \setminus E_1).$$

Step 2. $E_1 = (E_1 \setminus E_2) \cup (E_1 \cap E_2)$ and $E_2 = (E_2 \setminus E_1) \cup (E_1 \cap E_2)$, each a disjoint union. By (P2): $$\Pr(E_1 \setminus E_2) = \Pr(E_1) - \Pr(E_1 \cap E_2),$$ $$\Pr(E_2 \setminus E_1) = \Pr(E_2) - \Pr(E_1 \cap E_2).$$

Step 3. Substitute into Step 1: $$\begin{aligned}\Pr(E_1 \cup E_2) &= \big(\Pr(E_1) - \Pr(E_1 \cap E_2)\big) + \Pr(E_1 \cap E_2) \\ &\quad + \big(\Pr(E_2) - \Pr(E_1 \cap E_2)\big) \\ &= \Pr(E_1) + \Pr(E_2) - \Pr(E_1 \cap E_2).\end{aligned}$$ All the sets used are in $\sigma(\Omega)$, because a σ-algebra is closed under intersection and difference (Section 21).

Example. $E_1$ = “first coin Head” $= \{HH, HT\}$ and $E_2$ = “second coin Head” $= \{HH, TH\}$. Then $E_1 \cap E_2 = \{HH\}$ and

$$\Pr(E_1 \cup E_2) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 - \tfrac14 = \tfrac34,$$

which matches $\Pr(\{HH, HT, TH\}) = 3/4$ from the table in Section 24.

A special case is worth stating. If $E_1$ and $E_2$ are mutually exclusive, then $E_1 \cap E_2 = \emptyset$, $\Pr(E_1 \cap E_2) = 0$, and the rule reduces to $\Pr(E_1 \cup E_2) = \Pr(E_1) + \Pr(E_2)$, which is axiom (P2).

In real life · two detectors, one source

Suppose that in a short counting interval detector 1 registers a pulse with probability 0.30, detector 2 with probability 0.20, and both together (a coincidence) with probability 0.08. The probability that at least one registers is $0.30 + 0.20 - 0.08 = 0.42$, not $0.50$. The same arithmetic appears in everyday surveys: if 60% of students come by bus, 30% by metro and 10% use both, then $60 + 30 - 10 = 80\%$ use at least one of them. Forgetting to subtract the overlap is one of the most common errors in practice.

Check yourself

(a) $\Pr(E_1) = 0.5$, $\Pr(E_2) = 0.4$ and $\Pr(E_1 \cup E_2) = 0.7$. Find $\Pr(E_1 \cap E_2)$. (b) Explain why two events with $\Pr(E_1) = 0.7$ and $\Pr(E_2) = 0.6$ cannot be mutually exclusive.

(a) $0.5 + 0.4 - 0.7 = 0.2$. (b) If they were mutually exclusive, (P2) would give $\Pr(E_1 \cup E_2) = 1.3$, but no probability can exceed 1 (by monotonicity, $\Pr(E_1 \cup E_2) \le \Pr(\Omega) = 1$). So they must overlap, with $\Pr(E_1 \cap E_2) \ge 0.3$.

Chapter 4 Conditional probability and independence How a piece of information changes a probability, and when it does not.

Section 26Conditional probability

Suppose we are told that event $E$ has occurred, but not which outcome. How likely is $E'$ now?

A note on notation

In this chapter $E'$ is simply a second event, a different subset of $\Omega$. It is not the complement of $E$. (In Class 1 you saw $A'$ as one way of writing a complement; here the complement is always written $E^{c}$.)

Finite sets, equally likely outcomes

Once we know $E$ occurred, every outcome outside $E$ is ruled out. $E$ becomes the new sample space. Inside it, $E'$ can only occur through the outcomes in $E' \cap E$. So

$$\Pr(E' \mid E) = \frac{n(E' \cap E)}{n(E)} \qquad \text{read: “probability of } E' \text{ given } E\text{”}.$$

Dividing numerator and denominator by $n(\Omega)$ turns counts into probabilities:

$$\Pr(E' \mid E) = \frac{n(E' \cap E)/n(\Omega)}{n(E)/n(\Omega)} = \frac{\Pr(E' \cap E)}{\Pr(E)}.$$

General definition

The last expression makes sense in any probability space, with or without equally likely outcomes, so we take it as the definition.

Definition · conditional probability

For events $E', E \in \sigma(\Omega)$ with $\Pr(E) > 0$,

$$\Pr(E' \mid E) = \frac{\Pr(E' \cap E)}{\Pr(E)}.$$

The condition $\Pr(E) > 0$ is needed because we divide by $\Pr(E)$. It also makes sense: we cannot be “told” that an impossible event happened.

Worked example · both Heads, given at least one Head

Two tosses of an unbiased coin. $E'$ = “both Heads” $= \{HH\}$, $E$ = “at least one Head” $= \{HH, HT, TH\}$. Find $\Pr(E' \mid E)$.

Step 1. Throw away the outcomes outside $E$. Only $TT$ is ruled out; the new sample space is $\{HH, HT, TH\}$, three equally likely outcomes.

Step 2. Find the part of $E'$ that survives: $E' \cap E = \{HH\}$.

Step 3. Take the ratio: $$\Pr(E' \mid E) = \frac{n(\{HH\})}{n(\{HH, HT, TH\})} = \frac13.$$

Step 4. Compare: without the information, $\Pr(E') = 1/4$. Knowing $E$ changed the probability of $E'$ from $\tfrac14$ to $\tfrac13$. (Check with the formula: $\Pr(E' \cap E)/\Pr(E) = \tfrac14 \big/ \tfrac34 = \tfrac13$.)

Multiplication rule

Rearranging the definition, and writing it also with the roles of $E'$ and $E$ swapped (note $E' \cap E = E \cap E'$):

$$\boxed{\,\Pr(E \cap E') = \Pr(E \mid E')\,\Pr(E') = \Pr(E' \mid E)\,\Pr(E)\,}$$

This is the form we use most: the probability that both happen is the probability that one happens, times the probability that the other happens given the first. It is why we multiply along the branches of a tree diagram. The second-level branches carry conditional probabilities: in Figure 7, $\Pr(\{HH\}) = \Pr(\text{1st H}) \times \Pr(\text{2nd H} \mid \text{1st H}) = \tfrac12 \times \tfrac12 = \tfrac14$.

In real life · false alarms

A monitoring system checks a component many times. Use some round, made-up numbers: a real fault is present in 1 check out of 1000; when there is a fault, the alarm sounds 99% of the time; when there is no fault, the alarm still sounds (a false alarm) 2% of the time. The alarm sounds. How likely is a real fault?

Think of 100,000 checks (the tree below). There are 100 faulty checks, giving 99 alarms, and 99,900 healthy ones, giving 1,998 false alarms. Given that the alarm sounded, the new sample space is the $99 + 1998 = 2097$ alarms, and only 99 of them are real:

$$\Pr(\text{fault} \mid \text{alarm}) = \frac{99}{2097} \approx 0.047.$$

Fewer than 1 alarm in 20 is real, even though the detector catches 99% of faults. This is why safety systems often require two independent signals before acting, and why operators are trained to confirm an alarm with a second indication.

Common pitfall

$\Pr(E' \mid E)$ and $\Pr(E \mid E')$ are different numbers. In the alarm example, $\Pr(\text{alarm} \mid \text{fault}) = 0.99$, but $\Pr(\text{fault} \mid \text{alarm}) \approx 0.047$. The denominator is the event you were given, and swapping it changes everything.

Section 27Independence

Sometimes the information that $E'$ occurred is useless for predicting $E$: knowing it leaves the probability exactly where it was. Then the two events are called independent.

Definition · independent events

$E$ and $E'$ are independent if knowing that $E'$ occurred does not change the probability of $E$:

$$\Pr(E \mid E') = \Pr(E).$$

Putting this into the multiplication rule gives the equivalent (and symmetric) form:

$$\boxed{\,E, E' \text{ independent} \iff \Pr(E' \cap E) = \Pr(E')\,\Pr(E)\,}$$

The product form has two advantages. It is symmetric: it does not matter which event we call “given”. And it still makes sense when one of the events has probability zero, where $\Pr(E \mid E')$ is undefined. For that reason the product form is usually taken as the official definition.

Example. $E'$ = “first coin Head” $= \{HH, HT\}$ and $E$ = “second coin Head” $= \{HH, TH\}$. Then

$$\Pr(E' \cap E) = \Pr(\{HH\}) = \tfrac14 = \tfrac12 \times \tfrac12 = \Pr(E')\,\Pr(E).$$

Independent: what the first coin did tells us nothing about the second. This is also where the bent-coin numbers of Figure 6 came from. If the coin lands Heads with probability 0.7 and the two tosses are independent, then $\Pr(\{HH\}) = 0.7 \times 0.7 = 0.49$, $\Pr(\{HT\}) = 0.7 \times 0.3 = 0.21$, and so on.

In real life · redundancy and common-cause failure

A safety function is often given two redundant pumps so that it still works if one fails. Suppose each pump fails to start on demand with probability 0.01. If the two failures are independent, both fail with probability $0.01 \times 0.01 = 0.0001$, a hundred times smaller. Real safety analyses do not stop there. A shared cause (a common power supply, the same maintenance error made on both pumps, flooding of the room they stand in) makes the failures dependent, and then the probability that both fail can be much larger than the product. Probabilistic safety assessments therefore model common-cause failures explicitly. The multiplication is only as good as the independence assumption behind it.

In real life · accidental coincidences

In coincidence counting, two detectors each pick up random background pulses. If the two backgrounds are independent, the chance that both give a background pulse in the same short window is the product of their individual chances. This is why the rate of accidental (chance) coincidences is proportional to the product of the two detectors’ count rates, and why it can be estimated and subtracted.

27.1 Mutually exclusive is not the same as independent

These two ideas are often confused, but they are almost opposites. $E'$ and $E$ are mutually exclusive when $E' \cap E = \emptyset$. In that case $\Pr(E' \cap E) = 0$, so they are independent only if $\Pr(E')\Pr(E) = 0$, i.e. only if $\Pr(E') = 0$ or $\Pr(E) = 0$. Mutually exclusive events with non-zero probabilities are always dependent.

Example of mutually exclusive, dependent events. Take $E' = \{HH\}$ and $E = \{TT\}$, each with probability $1/4$. If we learn that $E$ occurred (both Tails), then $E'$ (both Heads) is now impossible:

$$\Pr(E' \mid E) = \frac{\Pr(\emptyset)}{\Pr(E)} = 0 \neq \tfrac14 = \Pr(E').$$

“They can’t both happen, so they must be independent” is wrong. If they can’t both happen, knowing one tells you a great deal about the other. Section 28Independence in daily life: counting routes

Question. From bus depot $A$ to depot $B$ there are 5 routes. From $B$ to depot $C$ there are 3 routes. How many ways are there to travel from $A$ to $C$ via $B$?

Answer. $5 \times 3 = 15$. Everyone “knows” this, but why multiplication? Each journey is a pair (route $A \to B$, route $B \to C$):

$$\Omega = \{r_1s_1,\ r_1s_2,\ r_1s_3,\ r_2s_1,\ \dots,\ r_5s_3\}, \qquad n(\Omega) = 5 \times 3 = 15.$$

The count is 15 only because every $B \to C$ route can follow every $A \to B$ route: the choice of the second route does not depend on the first. That is independence.

In the language of probability. Suppose a traveller picks the $A \to B$ route at random (each with probability $1/5$) and, independently, the $B \to C$ route at random (each $1/3$). Let $E_i$ = “first leg on $r_i$” and $E'_j$ = “second leg on $s_j$”. By independence,

$$\Pr\big(\{r_is_j\}\big) = \Pr(E_i \cap E'_j) = \Pr(E_i)\,\Pr(E'_j) = \frac15 \times \frac13 = \frac1{15}.$$

So there are 15 equally likely journeys. The multiplication rule of counting is the product rule for independent events in disguise.

Worked example · when independence fails

A bus on route $r_1$ always reaches $B$ after the last bus on route $s_3$ has left. How many journeys are possible now?

Step 1. The second leg now depends on the first. After $r_1$, only $s_1$ and $s_2$ are possible: 2 continuations.

Step 2. After any of $r_2, r_3, r_4, r_5$, all 3 continuations are still possible.

Step 3. Count the two groups separately and add: $$4 \times 3 + 1 \times 2 = 14,$$ not 15. Simple multiplication gives the wrong answer because the assumption behind it (every second leg can follow every first leg) is false.

We use independence like this all the time, often without noticing. The framework of this chapter lets us see exactly what we are assuming when we multiply, and when that assumption is wrong.

In real life · building a neutron history

A Monte Carlo code builds the probability of a whole neutron history step by step with the multiplication rule: the probability of this flight distance, times the probability of hitting this nuclide given where the collision happened, times the probability of this reaction given the nuclide and the neutron’s energy, and so on. Most factors are conditional probabilities, because what can happen next depends on what has happened so far (the neutron’s energy, the material it is in). Only when a step is independent of the earlier ones does its factor become a plain, unconditional probability, just as the route count becomes a plain product only when every second leg can follow every first leg.

Check yourself

A fourth route $s_4$ from $B$ to $C$ opens, but its buses connect only with routes $r_2$ and $r_3$. (Ignore the late-$r_1$ problem.) How many journeys from $A$ to $C$ are there now?

After $r_2$ or $r_3$: 4 continuations; after $r_1, r_4, r_5$: 3 continuations. Total $2 \times 4 + 3 \times 3 = 17$. Not $5 \times 4 = 20$, because the second choice now depends on the first.

Chapter 5 Random variables Turning abstract outcomes into real numbers, without losing the probabilities.

Section 29Random variable as a measurable function

The sample space and event space of probability theory contain abstract objects: strings like $HT$, or whole neutron histories. Working with these abstract objects is cumbersome. You cannot add two coin faces or average two histories. It is always better to work with real numbers, which we can add, average and plot. So we define a function

$$X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R},$$

that is, a function from the abstract sample space to the real number line. Each outcome $\omega$ gets a number $X(\omega)$. What properties must this function have?

Firstly, to preserve the structure of the event space, we make an analogous measurable space $(\mathbb{R}, \sigma(\mathbb{R}))$ on the number line, which is just like the space $(\Omega, \sigma(\Omega))$: $\sigma(\mathbb{R})$ is a σ-algebra of subsets of $\mathbb{R}$, the sets of numbers we want to ask about. (In practice $\sigma(\mathbb{R})$ is the Borel σ-algebra, the smallest σ-algebra containing all intervals. Every set you are likely to meet, such as an interval, a single point, or a union of these, belongs to it.)

The function $X$ need not be one-to-one: different outcomes may give the same value. In the example below both $HT$ and $TH$ give 1. Nor need it be onto: some real numbers, such as $3.7$, may never be produced. (Recall these words from Class 1, Sections 15 and 16.) But for every event $E' \in \sigma(\mathbb{R})$, the set of outcomes that $X$ sends into $E'$ must be an event in $\sigma(\Omega)$, so that $\Pr$ can measure it.

Definition · random variable

For a given probability space $(\Omega, \sigma(\Omega), \Pr)$ and measurable space $(\mathbb{R}, \sigma(\mathbb{R}))$, a function $X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ is called a random variable if, for any event $E' \in \sigma(\mathbb{R})$,

$$X^{-1}(E') = \{\omega \in \Omega \mid X(\omega) \in E'\} \in \sigma(\Omega).$$

(A function with this property is called measurable.)

Every set of numbers has a preimage, perhaps the empty set, and every preimage is automatically a subset of $\Omega$. The requirement is stronger: the preimage must be an event, a member of the chosen $\sigma(\Omega)$. Only then is its probability defined.

The probability of the values that $X$ can produce is determined by the probability of the preimage event. For any $E' \in \sigma(\mathbb{R})$,

$$\operatorname{Pr}_X(E') = \Pr\big(X^{-1}(E')\big) = \Pr\big(\{\omega \in \Omega \mid X(\omega) \in E'\}\big).$$

In particular, for a single value $x$ we write $\operatorname{Pr}_X(x)$ for

$$\operatorname{Pr}_X(x) = \operatorname{Pr}_X(\{x\}) = \Pr(X = x) = \Pr\big(\{\omega \in \Omega \mid X(\omega) = x\}\big).$$

Since the preimage is an event of the probability space $(\Omega, \sigma(\Omega), \Pr)$, its probability is well defined.

Key idea · two different functions

$\operatorname{Pr}_X$ “eats up” an event $E' \in \sigma(\mathbb{R})$, i.e. a set of real numbers, and produces a number in $[0, 1]$. It satisfies both axioms: $\operatorname{Pr}_X(\mathbb{R}) = \Pr(\Omega) = 1$, and preimages of mutually exclusive sets are mutually exclusive, so the probabilities add. Hence $(\mathbb{R}, \sigma(\mathbb{R}), \operatorname{Pr}_X)$ is itself a probability space, called the distribution of $X$.

The function $x \mapsto \operatorname{Pr}_X(x) = \Pr(X = x)$, on the other hand, eats up a single real number $x$ and produces a number in $[0, 1]$. It is called the probability mass function of $X$.

Does measurability ever fail? Yes, and our two-coin example shows how. Recall the small event space $\sigma_{HH}(\Omega) = \{\emptyset, \{HH\}, \{HT, TH, TT\}, \Omega\}$ from Section 22. With that event space, “number of Heads” is not a random variable, because the preimage of $\{1\}$ is $\{HT, TH\}$, which $\Pr$ cannot measure. Explore it below.

Common pitfall

A random variable is neither random nor a variable: it is a fixed, ordinary function. All the randomness lives in which outcome $\omega$ the experiment produces; $X$ just reports a number about it.

In real life · random variables in reactor physics

The number of neutrons released in one fission; the number of counts a detector records in one second; the energy of a neutron born in fission; the distance a neutron flies before its next collision. In each case the underlying outcome is a complicated physical event, but the random variable boils it down to one number that we can average, histogram and compare with measurement.

Check yourself

Why does the definition demand $X^{-1}(E') \in \sigma(\Omega)$ rather than just $X^{-1}(E') \subseteq \Omega$?

Because $X^{-1}(E') \subseteq \Omega$ is always true and so would demand nothing. What we need is to compute $\Pr(X \in E') = \Pr(X^{-1}(E'))$, and $\Pr$ is defined only on members of $\sigma(\Omega)$. So the preimage must be an event.

Section 32Mean (expectation) of a random variable

The first summary number answers “what value do we get on average?”. Each possible value counts in proportion to how likely it is: a probability-weighted average.

Definition · mean

The mean (expectation, expected value) of a discrete random variable $X$ is

$$\bar{X} = E[X] = \sum_i x_i \operatorname{Pr}_X(x_i).$$

It is also written $\mu$, $\mu_X$ or $\langle X \rangle$.

The mean need not be a value that $X$ can take: $5.6$ is not one of $2, 5, 7, 9$. “Expected value” does not mean “the value you should expect to see”; it means the long-run average. Reactor calculations (criticality, the multiplication factor) are built on such averages.

Section 33Variance and standard deviation

Two random variables can have the same mean and still behave very differently: one may always land close to its mean, the other may swing wildly from one side to the other. Another measure of the R.V. $X$ is therefore how far, typically, a number produced in one random experiment lies from the mean value.

We cannot simply average the deviations $x_i - \bar{X}$: the positive and negative ones cancel exactly (Section 34 proves that their average is always zero). So we square them first, which makes every deviation count as positive and makes large deviations count more.

Definition · variance and standard deviation

The variance of $X$, denoted $\sigma_X^2$, is

$$\sigma_X^2 = \sum_i (x_i - \bar{X})^2 \operatorname{Pr}_X(x_i).$$

The non-negative square root of the variance, $\sigma_X$, is called the standard deviation.

The variance is measured in squared units (if $X$ is in cm, $\sigma_X^2$ is in cm²). Taking the square root brings us back to the units of $X$, which is why the standard deviation is the easier number to picture.

The variance is zero only when all the probability sits on a single value, so that $X$ is not really random at all.

In real life · counting statistics

Every measured count rate carries a standard deviation. For the usual (Poisson) statistics of radioactive decay and neutron counting, a count of $N$ has a standard deviation of about $\sqrt{N}$. So 100 counts are uncertain by about $\pm 10$ (10%), while 10,000 counts are uncertain by about $\pm 100$ (1%). This is why detector measurements, and Monte Carlo tallies, are run long enough to collect many counts: the relative spread $\sqrt{N}/N = 1/\sqrt{N}$ shrinks only slowly. You will meet the Poisson distribution properly in a later class.

Section 34n-th moment of a random variable

The mean averages $x$; the variance averages $(x - \bar{X})^2$. Nothing stops us from averaging other powers. These averages are called moments.

Definition · central and raw moments

The $n$-th order central moment of $X$ is

$$\mu_n(X) = \sum_i (x_i - \bar{X})^n \operatorname{Pr}_X(x_i).$$

“Central” here means “measured from the mean value $\bar{X}$”. Another way to describe a R.V. is through its $n$-th order raw moment:

$$E[X^n] = \langle X^n \rangle = \sum_i x_i^n \operatorname{Pr}_X(x_i).$$

So the mean is the first raw moment, $E[X^1]$, and the variance is the second central moment, $\mu_2(X)$.

34.1 First central moment is always zero

Expanding the sum and using $\sum_i \operatorname{Pr}_X(x_i) = 1$:

$$\begin{aligned} \mu_1(X) &= \sum_i (x_i - \bar{X}) \operatorname{Pr}_X(x_i) \\ &= \Big[\sum_i x_i \operatorname{Pr}_X(x_i)\Big] - \bar{X} \sum_i \operatorname{Pr}_X(x_i) \\ &= \bar{X} - \bar{X} \cdot 1 = 0. \end{aligned}$$

This is the balance property you saw in Animation 9: the probability-weighted deviations on the left of the mean exactly cancel those on the right. (It is also why the variance uses squared deviations.)

34.2 Second central moment (variance) in terms of raw moments

Expand the square, split the sum into three, and pull out the constants:

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma_X^2 = \mu_2(X) &= \sum_i (x_i - \bar{X})^2 \operatorname{Pr}_X(x_i) \\ &= \sum_i \big(x_i^2 - 2\bar{X} x_i + \bar{X}^2\big) \operatorname{Pr}_X(x_i) \\ &= \Big[\sum_i x_i^2 \operatorname{Pr}_X(x_i)\Big] - 2\bar{X} \sum_i x_i \operatorname{Pr}_X(x_i) + \bar{X}^2 \sum_i \operatorname{Pr}_X(x_i) \\ &= E[X^2] - 2\bar{X} \cdot \bar{X} + \bar{X}^2 \cdot 1 \\ &= E[X^2] - \bar{X}^2. \end{aligned}$$

In words: variance = mean of the square − square of the mean. This is useful for calculating the variance of a probability distribution in one go, without first computing every deviation.

In real life · beyond the mean in reactor noise

The mean number of neutrons per fission, $\bar{\nu}$, fixes how a reactor’s neutron population grows on average. Reactor-noise methods such as the Feynman-α (variance-to-mean) method look at the fluctuations of a detector’s count, and those fluctuations depend on the second moment of the number of neutrons per fission as well, through $\langle \nu(\nu - 1) \rangle$. Higher moments carry physical information that the mean alone cannot.

Summary
  • A random experiment is described by a probability space $(\Omega, \sigma(\Omega), \Pr)$: outcomes, the events we may ask about, and their probabilities.
  • An event space (σ-algebra) contains $\Omega$ and is closed under complements and countable unions, hence also under intersections and differences. The power set is the largest; $\{\emptyset, \Omega\}$ is the smallest.
  • The axioms: $\Pr(\Omega) = 1$, and probabilities of mutually exclusive events add. From them: $\Pr(\emptyset) = 0$, monotonicity, $\Pr(E^{c}) = 1 - \Pr(E)$, and $\Pr(E_1 \cup E_2) = \Pr(E_1) + \Pr(E_2) - \Pr(E_1 \cap E_2)$.
  • Conditional probability $\Pr(E' \mid E) = \Pr(E' \cap E)/\Pr(E)$: the given event becomes the new sample space. Multiplication rule: $\Pr(E \cap E') = \Pr(E' \mid E)\Pr(E)$.
  • Independence: $\Pr(E' \cap E) = \Pr(E')\Pr(E)$. Mutually exclusive events with non-zero probabilities are always dependent. The counting rule “$5 \times 3 = 15$” is the product rule for independent choices.
  • A random variable is a measurable function $X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$: every $X^{-1}(E')$ must be an event. Its distribution is $\operatorname{Pr}_X(E') = \Pr(X^{-1}(E'))$, and its probability mass function is $\Pr(X = x)$.
  • Mean $E[X] = \sum_i x_i \operatorname{Pr}_X(x_i)$ (the long-run average, the balance point); variance $\sigma_X^2 = \sum_i (x_i - \bar{X})^2 \operatorname{Pr}_X(x_i) = E[X^2] - \bar{X}^2$; standard deviation $\sigma_X = \sqrt{\sigma_X^2}$; the first central moment is always 0.
