Sets and functions

Before we can follow a neutron through a reactor, we need a careful language for “what can happen”. This class builds it slowly: probability as counting, sets to hold the possible results, and functions to attach labels and numbers to them.

≈ 75 min read11 animationsBased on the Class 1 notes

By the end you will be able to

  • work out a probability as “favourable outcomes ÷ all outcomes”, and say when that rule can be trusted (no bias);
  • name the random experiment and its outcomes in a word problem;
  • read and write sets in roster and set-builder form, and use $\in$, $\subseteq$, $\emptyset$ and the power set $2^A$;
  • find unions, intersections, differences and complements, and recognise mutually exclusive sets;
  • count $n(A \cup B)$ without double counting, and use the distributive and De Morgan laws;
  • decide whether a rule is a function, and whether it is injective, surjective or bijective; explain why only bijections can be inverted; and find a preimage.
Part 0Introduction to probability

01Empirical probability

Many things in nature happen again and again, but not in exactly the same way each time. Toss a coin twice and you may see Head and then Tail. Hold a radiation detector next to a source for one second, then for another second, and the two counts will usually differ.

In such cases we cannot say for sure what the next result will be. What we can do is talk about how likely each result is. That “how likely” is a number called the probability.

The simplest way to measure it is plain counting. Count the ways the thing you are interested in can happen. Then divide by all the ways things can turn out.

The counting rule $$\Pr = \frac{\text{number of favourable results}}{\text{total number of possible results}}$$

Because the top can never be bigger than the bottom, and neither can be negative, the answer is always a number between 0 and 1. A probability of 0 means the result never happens. A probability of 1 means it always happens. Everything else lies in between.

Quick check

Toss one fair coin. What is the probability of a Head?

List the possible results: Head and Tail. That is 2 results.

Count the favourable ones: only Head, so 1.

Divide: $\Pr(\text{Head}) = 1/2$.

Notice what the rule needs

To use this rule at all, we need complete information about what the possible results are. If we do not know the full list, we cannot fill in the bottom of the fraction.

There is a second way to see the number $1/2$. Suppose you really toss the coin many times and keep a running score: “heads so far ÷ tosses so far”. Try it below.

In real life

Detector counts. Radioactive decay is random. If a detector records the number of counts from a steady source in one-second windows, the count changes from window to window even though nothing about the source has changed. Reactor instruments therefore report averages over a time window, and the longer the window, the steadier the reading — the same settling you just saw with the coin.

Check yourself

(a) A fair six-faced die is rolled. What is the probability of an even number? (b) Someone says the probability of rain tomorrow is 1.2. What is wrong?

(a) The possible results are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 (six of them); the favourable ones are 2, 4, 6 (three). So $\Pr = 3/6 = 1/2$. (b) A probability can never be larger than 1, because the favourable results are only some of all the results.

02Two words: “random experiment” and “outcome”

The word “result” above is a little loose. Probability uses two precise words instead. Let us meet them slowly.

Definition · Random experiment

A phenomenon that can be repeated, whose result we cannot predict with certainty before it is done, although we do know what results it could possibly give.

Tossing a coin, rolling a six-faced die, and checking the age of the next person you meet are all random experiments. Each can be repeated; you cannot say in advance what you will get; but you know the menu of possibilities.

Definition · Outcome

A single possible result of a random experiment.

Tossing one coin has two outcomes: Head (H) and Tail (T).

With these two words, the counting rule reads more precisely:

$$\Pr = \frac{\text{number of favourable outcomes of the experiment}}{\text{total number of outcomes of the experiment}}$$
In real life

Neutrons from one fission. When a uranium-235 nucleus fissions, it releases a small whole number of neutrons — sometimes none, more often two or three, occasionally more; for thermal fission the average is about 2.4. “Watch one fission and count the neutrons” is a random experiment. Its outcomes are the counts 0, 1, 2, 3, …. This one random experiment, repeated an enormous number of times every second, is what keeps a reactor running.

Check yourself

A quality inspector picks one fuel pellet from a tray of 500 pellets without looking. What is the random experiment, and what are its outcomes?

The random experiment is “pick one pellet from the tray”. Each of the 500 pellets is one outcome, so there are 500 outcomes.

03Worked example: meeting someone under 30

Let us use the two new words on an everyday question, one step at a time.

Worked example

Question. You walk out of your room. What is the probability that the next person you meet in the office is younger than 30? Suppose the office has 200 people, and 60 of them are younger than 30.

Random experiment: meeting the next person in the office.

Outcomes: each person working in the office is one possible outcome. So there are 200 outcomes.

Favourable outcomes: the people younger than 30. There are 60.

Divide: $$\Pr(\text{age} < 30) = \frac{60}{200} = 0.3$$

Read it back in words: roughly 3 out of every 10 people you meet should be under 30.

Below is a model of that office: four rooms of 50 people each, 200 in all. The 60 orange dots are the people under 30. Press Play and “meet” people one after another, each person equally likely to be the next one you meet.

04Bias: why the simple ratio is an ideal case

Look back at the worked example. The answer 0.3 quietly assumed something: every person in the office is equally likely to be the one you meet.

Is that true? Think of this person:

A person comes to the office, sits at his desk or lab the entire day, does not go to the canteen or the bank, and after office hours comes out and goes home. He will never meet another person who comes through another gate and sits in another office.

For him, colleagues in the same room are very likely to be met. People in the other building have a probability close to 0 of being met, however many of them there are. Counting heads in the whole office no longer describes his chances.

The ratio $60/200$ works only if every outcome has the same chance of occurring. That is an ideal case, and it rarely holds exactly in real life. When some outcomes are more likely than others for a systematic reason, the experiment is biased, and the simple ratio gives the wrong answer.

Definition · Bias

Bias is a systematic tendency of a random experiment to favour some outcomes over others. An experiment with no such tendency is unbiased: all its outcomes are equally likely.

Let us see bias happen. Now the person meeting others is our desk-bound colleague in Room 1. Room 1 happens to be a young lab: 30 of its 50 people are under 30, while each other room has only 10. The office as a whole still has 60 people under 30 out of 200.

Where does the long-run value come from?

This uses ideas from later classes, but the arithmetic is simple. Take $p = 0.9$.

90% of the time the person met is from Room 1, where the fraction under 30 is $30/50 = 0.6$.

10% of the time it is anyone in the office, where the fraction is $60/200 = 0.3$.

Mix them: $0.9 \times 0.6 + 0.1 \times 0.3 = 0.54 + 0.03 = 0.57$. In general $p(0.6) + (1-p)(0.3) = 0.3 + 0.3p$.

Common pitfall

A kind of outcome is not likely just because there are many outcomes of that kind. The 140 people in Rooms 2–4 are 70% of the office, yet they barely matter to our desk-bound colleague. Before you divide, always ask: is every outcome really equally likely?

In real life

Sampling for quality checks. Suppose an inspector always takes pellets for measurement from the top layer of a tray because it is convenient. If anything about the top layer differs systematically — for example, pellets placed last after a process change — the sample is biased, and the defect fraction it shows can differ from the true fraction in the whole lot. This is why sampling plans insist that every item has the same chance of being picked.

Everyday version. An opinion survey that only phones landline numbers mostly reaches the people who still have landlines. However many calls are made, the answer describes that group, not everyone.

Check yourself

A die has a small weight hidden under the face with 1 dot, so the 6 lands on top more often than the other faces. Is rolling it a biased experiment? Is $\Pr(6) = 1/6$?

Yes, it is biased: there is a systematic reason (the weight) that favours one face. So the counting rule $1/6$ does not apply; $\Pr(6)$ is larger than $1/6$, and we would have to measure it.

05What kind of thing is an outcome?

It is tempting to think outcomes are always numbers. They need not be.

  • Rolling a die gives a configuration of the die: it lands so that the top face shows a definite number of dots, 1 to 6.
  • Tossing a coin gives a configuration in which the top face is either head or tail.
  • Meeting a person gives… a person.

Example: tossing two coins

Here an outcome is a configuration: the way the two coins land. For instance, “HH” is the configuration in which both coins fall with their head on top. Writing the first coin’s face first, the four possible outcomes are:

OutcomeTop face of coin 1Top face of coin 2
HHHeadHead
HTHeadTail
THTailHead
TTTailTail

We collect all of them together and write $\{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$. To talk precisely about such collections of outcomes, and about events as groups of outcomes, we need a new language. That is the subject of the next chapter: set theory.

In real life

A neutron history. In a Monte Carlo transport calculation, the computer follows one neutron from birth until it is absorbed or escapes. The “outcome” of that experiment is not one number but a whole story: where it was born, each place it collided, what it hit, and how it ended. We will later attach numbers to such stories (for example, “did it leak?”) using functions, which is the last part of this class.

Check yourself

Three coins are tossed. List the outcomes. How many are there?

HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT — eight outcomes. Each coin has 2 faces, so there are $2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8$ configurations.

Chapter 1Set theory

06What is a set?

A set is the mathematician’s word for “a collection”. The only rule is that the collection must be clear: anyone should be able to decide, for any object, whether it is in or out.

Definition · Set

A set is a collection of well-defined objects.

“Well-defined” means that for any object we can say clearly whether it belongs to the collection or not.

  • “The faces of a coin” is a set: $\{H, T\}$.
  • “The tall students in this class” is not a set, because “tall” is not well defined. Is 175 cm tall? People will disagree.

The objects in a set are called its elements. We write

$$x \in A \quad \text{“}x \text{ is an element of } A\text{”}$$ $$x \notin A \quad \text{“}x \text{ is not an element of } A\text{”}$$

Sets are usually named with capital letters and elements with small letters, unless otherwise specified.

Venn diagram

A Venn diagram draws a set as a closed region, usually a circle. Each element is a point inside the region; anything outside the region is not in the set.

Roster form

The way we wrote the outcomes of two coin tosses, $\{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$, is called the roster form of a set: list every element, separated by commas, inside curly braces.

Key idea

In roster form the order does not matter and each element is listed once. So $\{H, T\}$ and $\{T, H\}$ are the same set.

In real life

“The fuel assemblies loaded into the core this cycle” is a set: each assembly either was loaded or was not. “The hot fuel pins” is not yet a set. It becomes one only when we fix a rule, such as “pins whose computed peak temperature exceeds a stated limit”. Engineering specifications are, in a sense, ways of making collections well defined.

Check yourself

Which of these are sets? (a) the even numbers between 1 and 9; (b) the good textbooks on reactor physics; (c) the outcomes of rolling one die.

(a) Yes: $\{2, 4, 6, 8\}$. (b) No: “good” is a matter of opinion, so membership is not well defined. (c) Yes: $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$.

07Finite and infinite sets, and cardinality

Some collections can be counted to the end; others go on forever.

Definition · Finite set

A set with a finite number of elements.

Examples: $\{H, T\}$; the outcomes of two coin tosses; the people in your office.

Definition · Infinite set

A set that has an infinite number of elements in it.

Examples: the natural numbers $\{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$; all real numbers between 0 and 1; the possible numbers of tosses needed to see the first Head (it could be $1, 2, 3, \dots$ — there is no largest possibility).

Definition · Cardinality

For a finite set $A$, the cardinality is the number of elements in $A$, written $n(A)$.

$$S = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\} \implies n(S) = 4$$
Key idea

The counting rule from Section 1 is really a ratio of cardinalities: $$\Pr(E) = \frac{n(E)}{n(S)},$$ where $S$ is the set of all outcomes and $E$ is the set of favourable ones. This only makes sense when $S$ is finite — we cannot divide by “infinity”.

For example, with two fair coins and $E$ = “at least one Head” $= \{HH, HT, TH\}$, we get $\Pr(E) = n(E)/n(S) = 3/4$.

In real life

A common pressurised-water-reactor fuel assembly is a 17 × 17 square lattice: 289 positions, of which 264 hold fuel rods, 24 are guide tubes for control rods and 1 is an instrumentation tube. The set of positions is finite with cardinality 289. By contrast, the set of possible positions of a neutron inside the core is infinite: position is a continuous quantity, like the real numbers between 0 and 1.

Check yourself

Finite or infinite? (a) the set of letters in the word REACTOR; (b) the set of possible counts a detector could record in one second, $\{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$. What is the cardinality of (a)?

(a) Finite: $\{R, E, A, C, T, O\}$. R appears twice in the word but is listed once, so the cardinality is 6. (b) Infinite: there is no largest count we can rule out in the model, even if huge counts are extremely unlikely.

08Null set, subset, superset, power set

Null set

Strange as it sounds, a collection with nothing in it is still a useful set.

Definition · Null set

The null set (empty set) is the set with no elements, written $\emptyset$ or $\{\,\}$. Its cardinality is $n(\emptyset) = 0$.

Example: the set of outcomes of two coin tosses that show three Heads. There are none, so it is $\emptyset$.

Subset and superset

Often we want a smaller collection taken from a bigger one: “the outcomes with at least one Head” out of all outcomes.

Definition · Subset, superset

$A$ is a subset of $B$, written $A \subseteq B$, if every element of $A$ is also an element of $B$. Equivalently, $B$ is a superset of $A$, written $B \supseteq A$.

$$A \subseteq B \iff (x \in A \implies x \in B)$$

In a Venn diagram, a subset is a region drawn entirely inside the larger region.

Useful facts:

  • Every set is a subset of itself: $A \subseteq A$.
  • The null set is a subset of every set: $\emptyset \subseteq A$. (There is no element of $\emptyset$ that could fail to be in $A$.)
  • If $A \subseteq B$ and $A \neq B$, then $A$ is a proper subset of $B$, written $A \subset B$.
Key idea

An event in probability is a subset of the set of outcomes. “At least one Head” is the subset $B$ above.

Power set

If events are subsets, it is natural to ask: what are all the events we could talk about? That is, what are all the subsets? Collect them into one big set.

Definition · Power set

The power set of $A$ is the set of all subsets of $A$. It is denoted $2^A$ (also $\mathcal{P}(A)$).

$$2^A = \{\, X \mid X \subseteq A \,\}$$

It is important to remember that $A$ itself is an element of $2^A$ (because $A \subseteq A$). The notes emphasise this with the picture

$$\begin{array}{c} 2^A \\ \downarrow \\ A \end{array}$$

which you can read as: “inside $2^A$, one of the elements is $A$ itself”, that is, $A \in 2^A$. The elements of $2^A$ are sets; the elements of $A$ are not (usually).

Worked example

List the power set of $A = \{a, b, c\}$.

Organise by size. Size 0: just $\emptyset$ (1 subset). Size 1: $\{a\}, \{b\}, \{c\}$ (3 subsets).

Size 2: $\{a, b\}, \{a, c\}, \{b, c\}$ (3 subsets). Size 3: $\{a, b, c\}$ (1 subset).

Size of subsetSubsetsCount
0$\emptyset$1
1$\{a\}, \{b\}, \{c\}$3
2$\{a, b\}, \{a, c\}, \{b, c\}$3
3$\{a, b, c\}$1

Collect them all: $$2^A = \{\, \emptyset,\ \{a\},\ \{b\},\ \{c\},\ \{a,b\},\ \{a,c\},\ \{b,c\},\ \{a,b,c\} \,\}$$ That is $1 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 8 = 2^3$ elements.

Why is there a 2 in the symbol of the power set?

Take a finite set $A$ of size $n$, i.e. $n(A) = n$. We can build its power set by deciding how many elements to put in the subset:

  • choosing no elements: $\binom{n}{0}$ such subsets;
  • choosing 1 element: $\binom{n}{1}$ such subsets;
  • choosing 2 elements: $\binom{n}{2}$ such subsets;
  • $\dots$
  • choosing all $n$ elements: $\binom{n}{n}$ such subsets.

So the number of elements in the power set is

$$\binom{n}{0}+\binom{n}{1}+\binom{n}{2}+\dots+\binom{n}{n} = 2^n.$$

Thus a finite set with $n$ elements has $2^n$ elements in its power set. Perhaps this is why the power set is written $2^A$.

The identity used above

The binomial expansion says $$(1+x)^n = \binom{n}{0} + \binom{n}{1} x + \binom{n}{2} x^2 + \dots + \binom{n}{n} x^n.$$ Put $x = 1$: the left side becomes $2^n$ and the right side becomes the sum of all the $\binom{n}{k}$.

There is also a quick way to see the $2^n$. Building a subset means walking through the elements and answering, for each one, “in or out?”. That is $n$ independent two-way switches, so $2 \times 2 \times \dots \times 2 = 2^n$ possible settings. The animation below flips the switches through every setting and files each subset by its size.

The power set is not limited to finite sets

The definition “the set of all subsets of $A$” makes sense for any set. For example, the power set of the natural numbers $\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$ contains $\emptyset$, $\{1\}$, $\{2, 4, 6, \dots\}$ (the even numbers), $\{1, 2, 3\}$ and $\mathbb{N}$ itself, among infinitely many others. However, since $\mathbb{N}$ is not finite, the counting rule $n(2^A) = 2^n$ no longer applies here.

In real life

A safety system has 4 independent detectors, each of which may be working or failed. A “failure pattern” is the subset of detectors that have failed. The possible patterns are exactly the elements of the power set: $2^4 = 16$ of them, from $\emptyset$ (all working) to the whole set (all failed). Reliability engineers enumerate these patterns when they ask which combinations still allow the system to act.

Check yourself

Let $D = \{d_1, d_2, d_3, d_4\}$. (a) How many elements does $2^D$ have? (b) How many subsets have exactly two elements? (c) True or false: $d_1 \in 2^D$.

(a) $2^4 = 16$. (b) $\binom{4}{2} = 6$: $\{d_1,d_2\}$, $\{d_1,d_3\}$, $\{d_1,d_4\}$, $\{d_2,d_3\}$, $\{d_2,d_4\}$, $\{d_3,d_4\}$. (c) False. The elements of $2^D$ are subsets of $D$. The subset $\{d_1\}$ is in $2^D$; the bare element $d_1$ is not.

09Set operations: union, intersection, difference

Just as $+$ and $\times$ build new numbers from old ones, set operations build a new set from two sets $A$ and $B$. To describe them precisely we use set-builder notation, which defines a set by the property its elements satisfy, instead of listing them:

$$\{\, x \mid \text{property of } x \,\} \qquad \text{read: “the set of all } x \text{ such that \dots”}$$

For example, $\{\, x \mid x \text{ is an even number between 1 and 9}\,\} = \{2, 4, 6, 8\}$.

Union

Think “pour both collections into one bucket”. The union $A \cup B$ contains every element that is in $A$, or in $B$, or in both.

$$x \in A \cup B \iff x \in A \lor x \in B$$ $$A \cup B = \{\, x \mid x \in A \lor x \in B \,\}$$

Intersection

Think “what the two collections share”. The intersection $A \cap B$ contains only the elements that are in both $A$ and $B$.

$$x \in A \cap B \iff x \in A \land x \in B$$ $$A \cap B = \{\, x \mid x \in A \land x \in B \,\}$$

Difference

Think “start with $A$ and throw away anything that is also in $B$”. The difference $A \setminus B$ (also written $A - B$) contains the elements of $A$ that are not in $B$.

$$x \in A \setminus B \iff x \in A \land x \notin B$$ $$A \setminus B = \{\, x \mid x \in A \land x \notin B \,\}$$
Order matters

In general $A \setminus B \neq B \setminus A$. Union and intersection do not care about order ($A \cup B = B \cup A$), but difference does.

Example with two coin tosses

Let $S = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$, $A$ = “first coin shows Head” $= \{HH, HT\}$, and $B$ = “second coin shows Head” $= \{HH, TH\}$.

OperationResultIn words
$A \cup B$$\{HH, HT, TH\}$at least one Head
$A \cap B$$\{HH\}$both Heads
$A \setminus B$$\{HT\}$only the first coin shows Head
$B \setminus A$$\{TH\}$only the second coin shows Head

Try every operation yourself. The explorer below can use the coin example or a bigger one with the numbers 1 to 20, where $A$ is the even numbers and $B$ is the multiples of 3.

In real life

Search filters are set operations. In a document database, “neutron AND detector” returns the intersection of the documents containing each word; “neutron OR detector” returns the union; “neutron NOT detector” returns the difference. The same logic is used when a plant engineer pulls “all work orders on pump P-1 that are not yet closed”.

Check yourself

Let $A = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ and $B = \{3, 4, 5\}$. Find $A \cup B$, $A \cap B$, $A \setminus B$ and $B \setminus A$.

$A \cup B = \{1,2,3,4,5\}$, $A \cap B = \{3, 4\}$, $A \setminus B = \{1, 2\}$, $B \setminus A = \{5\}$.

10Universal set, complement, and mutually exclusive sets

Universal set and complement

“Everything that is not $A$” only makes sense once we agree what “everything” is. That agreed background is the universal set.

Definition · Universal set

The universal set $U$ is the set of all objects under discussion. In probability, $U$ is the set of all outcomes $S$; for two coin tosses, $U = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$.

Definition · Complement

The complement of $A$, written $A^{c}$ (also $A'$ or $\bar{A}$), is the set of all elements of $U$ that are not in $A$.

$$A^{c} = \{\, x \mid x \in U \land x \notin A \,\} = U \setminus A$$

Example. With $A$ = “first coin shows Head” $= \{HH, HT\}$, the complement is $A^{c} = \{TH, TT\}$, “first coin shows Tail”.

Two facts follow at once:

$$A \cup A^{c} = U, \qquad A \cap A^{c} = \emptyset.$$

Together, $A$ and its complement cover everything, and they share nothing.

Mutually exclusive (disjoint) sets

Definition · Mutually exclusive

$A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive (disjoint) if they have no element in common: $$A \cap B = \emptyset.$$

Example. “Both Heads” $= \{HH\}$ and “Both Tails” $= \{TT\}$ are mutually exclusive: the two events cannot happen in the same toss. A set and its complement are always mutually exclusive.

In real life

Follow a single neutron until its history ends. In a simple picture it ends in exactly one of two ways: it is absorbed somewhere in the reactor, or it leaks out. These two events are mutually exclusive — the same neutron cannot do both — and, together, they cover every possibility, so each is the complement of the other. This is why designers can speak of a “non-leakage probability”: it is the probability of the complement of leakage.

Check yourself

Roll one die, $U = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$. Let $E$ = “even” and $L$ = “less than 3”. Find $E^{c}$ and $L^{c}$. Are $E$ and $L$ mutually exclusive?

$E = \{2,4,6\}$ so $E^{c} = \{1,3,5\}$. $L = \{1,2\}$ so $L^{c} = \{3,4,5,6\}$. $E \cap L = \{2\} \neq \emptyset$, so they are not mutually exclusive: rolling a 2 makes both happen.

11Counting the elements of a union

Probability by counting needs $n(\cdot)$ of events, and events are often built with $\cup$. So we need a reliable way to count a union.

Mutually exclusive finite sets

If $A$ and $B$ are finite and mutually exclusive, no element is counted twice, so the counts simply add:

$$A \cap B = \emptyset \implies n(A \cup B) = n(A) + n(B)$$

Example. $n(\{HH\} \cup \{TT\}) = n(\{HH, TT\}) = 2 = 1 + 1$.

Overlapping sets

If $A$ and $B$ overlap, $n(A) + n(B)$ counts the common elements twice — once as members of $A$, once as members of $B$. The fix is to break $A \cup B$ into pieces that do not overlap and then use the rule above.

Derivation, step by step

We want a formula for $n(A \cup B)$ in terms of $n(A)$, $n(B)$ and $n(A \cap B)$.

Step 1. Split the union into three mutually exclusive pieces: $$A \cup B = (A \setminus B) \cup (A \cap B) \cup (B \setminus A)$$ Since the pieces are disjoint, their counts add: $$n(A \cup B) = n(A \setminus B) + n(A \cap B) + n(B \setminus A)$$

Step 2. Split $A$ and $B$ the same way. $A = (A \setminus B) \cup (A \cap B)$ and $B = (B \setminus A) \cup (A \cap B)$, each a disjoint union. So $$n(A \setminus B) = n(A) - n(A \cap B), \qquad n(B \setminus A) = n(B) - n(A \cap B)$$

Step 3. Substitute into Step 1: $$n(A \cup B) = \big(n(A) - n(A \cap B)\big) + n(A \cap B) + \big(n(B) - n(A \cap B)\big)$$ One $n(A \cap B)$ cancels, leaving $$\boxed{\,n(A \cup B) = n(A) + n(B) - n(A \cap B)\,}$$

Check. When $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive, $n(A \cap B) = 0$ and we get back the first rule.

Example. $A = \{HH, HT\}$, $B = \{HH, TH\}$, $A \cap B = \{HH\}$. Then $n(A \cup B) = 2 + 2 - 1 = 3$, which matches $A \cup B = \{HH, HT, TH\}$.

In real life

An inspection report on a batch of fuel rods says 12 rods were flagged for a dimension check, 8 for a weld check, and 3 of them for both. How many distinct rods need rework? Not $12 + 8 = 20$: the 3 rods flagged twice would be counted twice. The answer is $12 + 8 - 3 = 17$ rods.

Check yourself

In a class of 40 students, 25 take a maths elective, 20 take a physics elective, and 10 take both. How many take at least one of the two? How many take neither?

$n(M \cup P) = 25 + 20 - 10 = 35$ take at least one. Neither is the complement: $40 - 35 = 5$ students.

12Union as addition, intersection as multiplication

There is a useful analogy between set operations and ordinary algebra. Union “puts together”, a bit like adding. Intersection “keeps only what both have”, a bit like multiplying.

AlgebraSet theory
addition, $a + b$union, $A \cup B$
multiplication, $a \times b$intersection, $A \cap B$

Using the analogy: the distributive law

In algebra, multiplication distributes over addition:

$$a \times (b + c) = (a \times b) + (a \times c)$$

Replace $\times$ by $\cap$ and $+$ by $\cup$, and we get the corresponding law for sets:

Distributive law $$A \cap (B \cup C) = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C)$$
A memory aid, not an exact match

For example, $A \cup A = A$, whereas $a + a = 2a$. Always check a guessed law with a Venn diagram.

That is exactly what the animation does. It builds the left side and the right side separately, step by step, and then compares the two shaded regions. The second law in the selector has no counterpart in ordinary algebra — $a + bc$ is not $(a+b)(a+c)$ — yet it is true for sets. The analogy can mislead in both directions.

Check yourself

Check the distributive law with $A = \{1,2,3,4\}$, $B = \{3,4,5\}$, $C = \{4,6\}$.

Left: $B \cup C = \{3,4,5,6\}$, so $A \cap (B \cup C) = \{3,4\}$. Right: $A \cap B = \{3,4\}$ and $A \cap C = \{4\}$, so $(A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C) = \{3,4\}$. Both sides agree.

13De Morgan's laws

Start with ordinary speech. “I will have neither tea nor coffee” means “not (tea or coffee)”, and it says the same as “no tea, and no coffee”. The “or” turned into an “and” when the “not” went inside. That is De Morgan’s law.

De Morgan's laws

Taking the complement turns a union into an intersection, and an intersection into a union:

$$(A \cup B)^{c} = A^{c} \cap B^{c}$$ $$(A \cap B)^{c} = A^{c} \cup B^{c}$$
Worked example

Check both laws with two coins: $A = \{HH, HT\}$ (first Head), $B = \{HH, TH\}$ (second Head), $U = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$.

$A \cup B = \{HH, HT, TH\}$, so $(A \cup B)^{c} = \{TT\}$ — “no Head at all”.

$A^{c} = \{TH, TT\}$ and $B^{c} = \{HT, TT\}$, so $A^{c} \cap B^{c} = \{TT\}$. The first law holds.

$A \cap B = \{HH\}$, so $(A \cap B)^{c} = \{HT, TH, TT\}$ — “not both Heads”.

$A^{c} \cup B^{c} = \{TH, TT\} \cup \{HT, TT\} = \{HT, TH, TT\}$. The second law holds.

In real life

Suppose a protection channel trips if signal 1 is high or signal 2 is high. When is the channel quiet? De Morgan answers immediately: “not (1 high or 2 high)” = “1 not high and 2 not high”. Engineers use exactly this rewriting to turn “trip” logic into “permissive” logic and back when they check circuit designs.

Check yourself

Write the complement of “the pump is running and the valve is open” without using the word “not” in front of a bracket.

By $(A \cap B)^{c} = A^{c} \cup B^{c}$: “the pump is not running, or the valve is not open” (or both).

Chapter 2Functions

14What is a function?

A function is a rule that takes each element of one set and gives back exactly one element of another set. Think of a machine: you put in an element of $A$, and one element of $B$ comes out — always one, never zero, never two.

Definition · Function

A function $f$ from a set $A$ to a set $B$ (written $f: A \to B$) is a relation that assigns to each element $a \in A$ exactly one element $b \in B$. We write this as $f(a) = b$ or $a \mapsto b$, where $a \in A$ and $b \in B$.

Two core criteria make something a function:

  • Total coverage: it must cover all elements of $A$. Every $a \in A$ must have a mapped image in $B$.
  • Single value assignment: one-to-many mappings are not allowed. An element $a \in A$ cannot map to more than one element of $B$.

Now build your own. Click an element of $A$, then an element of $B$, to draw an arrow (click the same pair again to remove it). The panel underneath judges your diagram against everything in this chapter, including the next three sections.

In real life

“Each person ↦ their date of birth” is a function: everyone has exactly one birth date, and many people share one. “Each person ↦ their phone number” is not a function in general: some people have two numbers (one-to-many), and some have none (coverage fails). In a reactor, “each fuel rod ↦ the assembly it sits in” is a function, since every rod is in exactly one assembly.

Check yourself

Let $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$ and $B = \{x, y\}$. Is the rule $1 \mapsto x,\ 2 \mapsto x,\ 3 \mapsto x$ a function? Is $1 \mapsto x,\ 1 \mapsto y,\ 2 \mapsto y,\ 3 \mapsto x$?

The first is a function: each element of $A$ has exactly one image (they happen to be the same; $y$ is not used — both are allowed). The second is not: 1 is sent to two elements.

15One-to-one (injective) functions

Some functions never “merge” two inputs: different inputs always land on different outputs. No element of $B$ is hit by two arrows.

Definition · Injective

A function $f: A \to B$ is injective (one-to-one) if distinct elements in $A$ map to distinct elements in $B$: $$f(a_1) = f(a_2) \implies a_1 = a_2.$$

Read the formula as a test: if two inputs ever give the same output, they must have been the same input all along.

In real life

“Each employee ↦ their employee ID” is injective: no two employees share an ID, so an ID points back to one person. Unused ID numbers (elements of $B$ with no arrow) are perfectly fine. Serial numbers stamped on fuel assemblies work the same way, which is what makes each assembly traceable.

Check yourself

Is $f(x) = x^2$ injective on $A = \{-2, -1, 0, 1, 2\}$? Is it injective on $A = \{0, 1, 2\}$?

On $\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}$: no, since $f(-1) = f(1) = 1$ with $-1 \neq 1$. On $\{0,1,2\}$: yes, the outputs $0, 1, 4$ are all different.

16Onto (surjective) functions

Injective was about $B$ receiving at most one arrow. Surjective is the opposite worry: does every element of $B$ receive at least one?

Definition · Surjective

A function $f: A \to B$ is surjective (onto) if every element in $B$ is covered by at least one element in $A$: for every $b \in B$ there exists $a \in A$ with $f(a) = b$, i.e. $\forall b \in B\ \exists a \in A : f(a) = b$.

Key idea

Injective: every $b$ gets at most one arrow. Surjective: every $b$ gets at least one arrow. The two properties are independent: a function can have either, both, or neither.

In real life

“Each student ↦ their tutorial section” is surjective if no section is empty, and it is far from injective, since many students share a section. In a calculation, “each neutron history ↦ how it ended (absorbed or leaked)” is usually surjective once enough histories are run, because both endings occur.

Check yourself

$A = \{1,2,3,4\}$, $B = \{\text{odd}, \text{even}\}$, $f$ sends each number to its parity. Injective? Surjective?

Surjective: “odd” is hit by 1 and 3, “even” by 2 and 4. Not injective: 1 and 3 share the output “odd”.

17Bijective functions and invertibility

Definition · Bijective

A function is bijective if it is both injective and surjective, forming a perfect one-to-one correspondence: every element of $B$ gets exactly one arrow.

Argument for invertibility

An inverse function $f^{-1}: B \to A$ reverses the direction of every arrow. But the reversed diagram must itself be a function from $B$ to $A$, so it must pass the two criteria of Section 14:

  1. Total coverage of the new domain $B$. If $f$ is not surjective, some element of $B$ has no arrow pointing to it. Reversing the arrows leaves this element with nothing to map to in $f^{-1}$, violating total coverage.
  2. No one-to-many mappings. If $f$ is not injective (i.e. it is many-to-one), several elements of $A$ point to the same element of $B$. Reversing the arrows forces that element of $B$ to map to several elements of $A$, which is an invalid one-to-many mapping.
Conclusion

Hence only bijective functions have a well-defined inverse function.

In real life

In a fully loaded core, “each fuel assembly ↦ its core position” is a bijection: every assembly has one position, and every position holds one assembly. That is why a loading map can be read in both directions — from position to assembly, and from assembly to position. If one position were empty (not surjective), the reverse question “which assembly is at that position?” would have no answer.

Check yourself

Does $f: \{1,2,3\} \to \{2,4,6\}$, $f(x) = 2x$, have an inverse? If so, what is it?

Yes. The outputs $2, 4, 6$ are all different (injective) and cover all of $B$ (surjective), so $f$ is bijective. The inverse is $f^{-1}(y) = y/2$: $2 \mapsto 1$, $4 \mapsto 2$, $6 \mapsto 3$.

18Preimage

Even when a function cannot be reversed, we can still ask a backward question: which inputs led here?

Consider a surjective but non-injective function $f: A \to B$. Since it is surjective, every element of $B$ is mapped to. Pick one such element $b \in B$ and ask: what are the elements of $A$ that are mapped to $b$? These elements form a set, called the preimage of $b$.

Definition · Preimage

The preimage of $b \in B$ under $f$ is $$f^{-1}(b) = \{\, x \in A \mid f(x) = b \,\} \subseteq A.$$

Same symbol, different meaning

Here $f^{-1}(b)$ is a set of inputs. It does not claim that an inverse function exists — we just saw that a non-injective $f$ has none. When $f$ is bijective, the preimage of each $b$ contains exactly one element, and that element is the inverse function’s value.

Here is a probability flavoured example. Roll two dice. The set of outcomes has 36 elements, the ordered pairs $(i, j)$. Let $f$ be “the sum of the two faces”, a function onto $\{2, 3, \dots, 12\}$. Pick a sum and see its preimage.

In real life

A Monte Carlo code runs, say, $N$ neutron histories. Let $f$ send each history to the way it ended: “absorbed” or “leaked”. The preimage $f^{-1}(\text{leaked})$ is the set of histories that leaked, and the code’s estimate of the leakage probability is $n(f^{-1}(\text{leaked}))/N$. A tally in such a code is essentially a preimage that is counted (often with weights).

Check yourself

Let $f(x) = x^2$ from $A = \{-2,-1,0,1,2\}$ to $B = \{0,1,2,3,4\}$. Find $f^{-1}(4)$, $f^{-1}(0)$ and $f^{-1}(3)$.

$f^{-1}(4) = \{-2, 2\}$, $f^{-1}(0) = \{0\}$. No $x$ in $A$ has $x^2 = 3$, so $f^{-1}(3) = \emptyset$. (This $f$ is not surjective; the definition still works and simply gives the empty set.)

Wrap-upSummary

Summary
  • For a random experiment with finitely many, equally likely outcomes, $\Pr(E) = n(E)/n(S)$, always between 0 and 1. If the experiment is biased, this ratio is wrong.
  • A random experiment can be repeated, has a known list of possible results, and an unpredictable actual result. An outcome is one possible result; it need not be a number.
  • A set is a well-defined collection, written in roster form $\{\dots\}$ or set-builder form $\{x \mid \dots\}$. Cardinality $n(A)$ counts a finite set.
  • $\emptyset \subseteq A \subseteq A$; an event is a subset of $S$; the power set $2^A$ holds all subsets, and has $2^n$ elements when $n(A) = n$.
  • $\cup$ (or), $\cap$ (and), $\setminus$ (but not), and $A^{c} = U \setminus A$. Disjoint means $A \cap B = \emptyset$.
  • $n(A \cup B) = n(A) + n(B) - n(A \cap B)$. Also $A \cap (B \cup C) = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C)$, $(A \cup B)^c = A^c \cap B^c$, $(A \cap B)^c = A^c \cup B^c$.
  • A function sends every element of $A$ to exactly one element of $B$. Injective: no two inputs share an output. Surjective: every output is hit. Bijective: both, and only bijections have an inverse.
  • The preimage $f^{-1}(b) = \{x \in A \mid f(x) = b\}$ is a subset of $A$; counting preimages is how we will turn outcomes into probabilities of numerical results.
