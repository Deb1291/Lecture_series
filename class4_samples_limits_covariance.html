Samples, limit theorems, sigma levels and covariance

In the lab we never see a distribution, only a handful of measured numbers. This class shows what those numbers can tell us: why averaging works, how big an error bar is, how to say “how rare” in sigmas, and how uncertainties travel through a calculation, which is the toolkit behind every Monte Carlo tally in neutron transport.

By the end you will be able to

  • tell a population from a sample, and a parameter from a statistic;
  • compute a sample mean and a sample variance, and explain why the variance divides by $N-1$;
  • explain why even a perfect experiment gives scattered results (statistical uncertainty);
  • state the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem, and say what each one does and does not promise;
  • quote a mean with its standard error $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$, and use the $\sqrt{N}$ law to plan a measurement or a Monte Carlo run;
  • turn a deviation into “how many sigmas” and read off one-sided and two-sided tail probabilities;
  • work with joint distributions, marginals, covariance, correlation and the covariance matrix;
  • propagate uncertainties through a formula, including correlations, with the first-order formula and the sandwich rule $V_y = J V_x J^{\mathsf T}$.

Before we startWhere we are going

Up to now we have assumed that we know the distribution of a random variable: its pmf $\operatorname{Pr}_X(x)$ or PDF $p_X(x)$, its mean and its variance.

In the laboratory it is the other way round. We do not know the distribution. We only have a handful of measured numbers in a logbook.

This class connects the two worlds. Here is the route we will take:

  1. We separate the population (the distribution) from the sample (the numbers we actually measured), and see why even a perfect experiment gives scattered numbers.
  2. Two theorems, the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem, tell us what a sample can say about its population.
  3. We measure how rare an event is in units of $\sigma$. This is the language behind the six sigma and five sigma ideas of Class 3 (Section 47).
  4. We extend the PDF to several random variables at once, define the covariance, and use it to propagate uncertainties.
Chapter 10 Population, sample and statistical uncertainty

Section 49Population and sample

Let us begin with two words that are easy to mix up.

Definition

Population: the complete collection of all values that could be observed in a random experiment, together with how often each occurs. Mathematically, the population is the probability distribution of the random variable, $\operatorname{Pr}_X(x)$ or $p_X(x)$.

Sample: the values that were actually observed, $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_N$. The number $N$ is the sample size.

Sometimes the population is a real, finite collection of objects: every pellet in a batch, every voter in a constituency.

Sometimes it is only conceptual: “all the results this measurement could ever give if we repeated it forever”. In physics and engineering it is almost always this second kind.

Here are some pairs to make the idea concrete.

In real life · quality control

A fuel fabrication line produces pellets in large batches. Checking every pellet in detail would be slow and expensive, so the plant measures a sample and uses it to judge the whole batch.

Everything in this class (how close the sample average is to the batch average, how big the error bar is, how rare an out-of-tolerance pellet is) is what makes that judgement trustworthy. The same logic lets a pollster speak about a whole constituency after interviewing a few thousand people.

A random sample

We will always assume that the sample is random: each $x_i$ is the result of an independent repetition of the same random experiment.

It helps to think about the moment before we measure. At that moment the $i$-th reading is not yet a number. In the language of Class 2 (Sections 27 and 29) it is a random variable $X_i$.

The variables $X_1, \dots, X_N$ are independent, and each has the same distribution as $X$. This is abbreviated i.i.d.: independent and identically distributed.

After we measure, each $X_i$ has taken a definite value $x_i$. So: capital letters are random variables, small letters are the numbers we wrote in the logbook.

Parameters and statistics

A number that describes the population, such as its mean or its standard deviation, is a parameter. It is a fixed number, but usually we do not know it.

A number computed from the sample is a statistic. A different sample gives a different value, so a statistic is itself a random variable.

A note on notation

In Classes 2 and 3 we wrote the mean of a random variable as $\bar{X}$. That is the population mean. From now on we also call it $\mu$, so $\mu = \bar{X} = E[X]$ and $\sigma = \sigma_X$.

Many textbooks write the sample mean as $\bar{x}$. To avoid any confusion with our $\bar{X}$, these notes write it as $\hat{\mu}_N$. The hat means “estimate of”, and the subscript reminds us how many values went into it.

Check yourself

You are about to record fifty one-second counts from a source. Which of these are random? (a) the population mean $\mu$ of the counts; (b) the sample mean $\hat\mu_{50}$ you will compute once the counting is done; (c) the number $7.42$ that a colleague computed yesterday from her own fifty counts.

(a) Not random: $\mu$ is a parameter, a fixed property of the source, even though we do not know it.

(b) Random: before the counting, $\hat\mu_{50}$ is a statistic, a function of the random variables $X_1,\dots,X_{50}$. Another run would give another value.

(c) Not random any more: $7.42$ is the value that her statistic happened to take. Capital letters before, small letters (and plain numbers) after.

Section 50Sample mean and sample variance

From a sample $x_1, \dots, x_N$ we compute two summary numbers: where the values sit, and how much they spread.

Definition $$\text{Sample mean:}\quad \hat{\mu}_N = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i$$ $$\text{Sample variance:}\quad s_N^2 = \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left(x_i - \hat{\mu}_N\right)^2$$

The sample standard deviation is $s_N = \sqrt{s_N^2}$.

Compare these with the population formulas of Class 2 (Sections 32 and 33). There, each possible value was weighted by its probability:

$$\mu = \sum_i x_i \operatorname{Pr}_X(x_i), \qquad \sigma^2 = \sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2 \operatorname{Pr}_X(x_i).$$

Here, each measured value gets the same weight $1/N$, because each was observed once.

Class 2 (Section 32) already showed why the two agree for big samples. If a value has probability $\operatorname{Pr}_X(x_i)$, it turns up about $N\operatorname{Pr}_X(x_i)$ times in $N$ repetitions. So the sample average approaches $\sum_i x_i \operatorname{Pr}_X(x_i)$. The law of large numbers (Section 52) makes this precise.

Worked example · five counts

The first five one-second counts from the caesium source of Section 51 were $7,\ 11,\ 2,\ 6,\ 12$. Find $\hat\mu_5$, $s_5^2$ and $s_5$.

Step 1: the mean. Add them up and divide by $N = 5$:

$$\hat\mu_5 = \frac{7 + 11 + 2 + 6 + 12}{5} = \frac{38}{5} = 7.6 .$$

Step 2: the deviations from the sample mean:

$$x_i - \hat\mu_5:\quad -0.6,\ \ 3.4,\ \ -5.6,\ \ -1.6,\ \ 4.4 .$$

A handy check: deviations from the sample mean always add up to zero. Here $-0.6 + 3.4 - 5.6 - 1.6 + 4.4 = 0$. ✓

Step 3: square and add.

$$0.36 + 11.56 + 31.36 + 2.56 + 19.36 = 65.2 .$$

Step 4: divide by $N - 1 = 4$.

$$s_5^2 = \frac{65.2}{4} = 16.3, \qquad s_5 = \sqrt{16.3} = 4.04 \text{ counts}.$$

(Dividing by $N = 5$ instead would have given $13.04$.)

Step 5: compare. The population here is $\mathrm{Poisson}(7.3)$, with $\sigma^2 = 7.3$. Our $s_5^2 = 16.3$ is far off. That is not a mistake: five values is a tiny sample, and its statistics jitter a lot. With all fifty counts (Section 51) we will get $s_{50} = 2.76$, close to $\sigma = 2.70$.

Why $N-1$ and not $N$?

Here is the intuition. The deviations are measured from $\hat{\mu}_N$, and $\hat{\mu}_N$ was itself calculated from the same data. So it sits right in the middle of the data.

In fact, the sample mean is the number that makes the sum of squared deviations as small as possible. Any other centre, including the true $\mu$, gives a bigger sum.

Deviations from $\hat{\mu}_N$ are therefore, on average, a little smaller than deviations from the true $\mu$. Dividing by $N-1$ instead of $N$ corrects exactly for this, so that the average of $s_N^2$ over many samples equals $\sigma^2$.

Another way to say it: the $N$ deviations must add to zero, so only $N - 1$ of them are free. We state the result without proof here (if you are curious, a short argument is set as an exercise in Section 58). For $N$ larger than about $20$ the difference hardly matters.

In real life · which standard deviation did you compute?

Pocket calculators often have two standard-deviation keys, labelled for example $\sigma_n$ and $\sigma_{n-1}$, or $\sigma_x$ and $s_x$. Spreadsheets have STDEV.S (divides by $N-1$) and STDEV.P (divides by $N$).

In Python, NumPy's np.std divides by $N$ unless you pass ddof=1, while pandas' .std() divides by $N-1$ by default. With five data points the two answers differ by about 12%, so it is worth knowing which one you used.

Check yourself

A sample of two readings: $9$ and $11$. Compute $\hat\mu_2$, and the variance both ways (dividing by $N-1$ and by $N$). On average over many such samples, which version equals $\sigma^2$?

$\hat\mu_2 = 10$. The deviations are $-1$ and $+1$, so the sum of squares is $2$. Dividing by $N-1 = 1$ gives $s_2^2 = 2$; dividing by $N = 2$ gives $1$.

Only the $N-1$ version is right on average. The $N$ version is, on average, $\tfrac{N-1}{N}\sigma^2 = \tfrac12\sigma^2$ for $N=2$: half the true value. (One sample can of course be off either way; “on average” is what matters.)

Section 51Statistical uncertainty: randomness built into the experiment

Why does a measurement, repeated under the same conditions, give different numbers? Engineers usually list sources such as:

  • instrument uncertainty: finite resolution, calibration error, electronic drift;
  • methodological error: the procedure itself, for example sample positioning, detector dead time, or a background that was not fully subtracted;
  • modelling error: the formula used to turn raw readings into the final quantity is only approximate;
  • fundamental limits: limits set by nature on how well a quantity can be known, even in principle.

Now imagine an ideal experiment in which all of these are absent: a perfect detector, a perfect procedure, an exact model.

In many experiments the results still scatter. The reason is that the phenomenon being measured is itself random.

A radioactive nucleus does not decide in advance when it will decay. A neutron reaching a sample may or may not scatter into our detector. Class 3 (Section 40) showed that, for a long-lived source with many nuclei, the number of decays counted in a fixed time window is then $\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$.

So repeating the measurement does not reproduce the same number. It draws a new value from the population distribution.

Key idea · statistical uncertainty

When the quantity measured is itself a random variable, the results of repeated measurements follow its probability distribution, even with no instrument, method, model or fundamental error at all. The standard deviation $\sigma$ of that distribution is non-zero. It is called the statistical uncertainty of a single measurement.

Other experiments with built-in randomness

  • Neutron scattering: each incident neutron scatters into a given detector pixel with a small probability, so pixel counts are Poisson.
  • Photon detection (shot noise): photons arrive at random times, so a photodiode current fluctuates.
  • Thermal (Johnson) noise in a resistor: random motion of electrons gives a fluctuating voltage.
  • Monte Carlo neutron transport codes: each simulated history is random, so every computed tally (flux, $k_{\text{eff}}$) comes with a statistical uncertainty.
In real life · Monte Carlo transport

In a Monte Carlo transport code every neutron history is a small random experiment. Where the neutron is born, how far it flies, which nucleus it hits and what happens next are all drawn from probability distributions.

So every tally (a flux in a cell, a reaction rate, $k_{\text{eff}}$) is a sample mean over histories, and the code prints a statistical uncertainty next to it, usually as a relative error. Run the same input with a different random-number seed and you get a slightly different answer. That is not a bug: it is exactly the statistical uncertainty of this section.

Two properties of statistical uncertainty

Two things set it apart from the other sources in the list above:

  1. It cannot be removed by building a better instrument. It is a property of the population, not of the apparatus.
  2. It can be reduced by collecting more data: count for longer, or average more repetitions. How fast it shrinks is the subject of the next chapter. A calibration error, by contrast, is the same in every repetition and does not average away.
Common pitfall

A small statistical uncertainty is not the same as a small total uncertainty. A thermocouple that reads 2 °C high reads 2 °C high every time. Averaging a thousand of its readings gives a very precise, wrong temperature.

Check yourself

(a) A colleague proposes buying a counter with better energy resolution to reduce the scatter of repeated one-second counts from a weak source. Will it help? (b) What will?

(a) No. The scatter $\sigma = \sqrt{\lambda}$ comes from the random decays of the source, not from the counter. Better resolution does not change $\lambda$.

(b) More data: count for longer, or average more one-second counts. (A detector that catches a larger fraction of the decays also helps, because it raises $\lambda$ itself and the relative scatter $\sqrt\lambda/\lambda = 1/\sqrt\lambda$ falls. That is also “more data”.)

Chapter 11 Two limit laws

In this chapter $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_N$ is a random sample (i.i.d.) from a population with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, and $\hat{\mu}_N = (X_1 + X_2 + \dots + X_N)/N$ is the sample mean, now regarded as a random variable (before the measurements are made). The two laws are stated without proof; the proofs are beyond the scope of this course. But we can watch them at work.

Section 52The law of large numbers

Imagine averaging more and more readings. Each new reading is one voice in a growing crowd, so it nudges the average less and less. Intuitively, the average should settle down.

The law of large numbers says it settles in exactly the right place.

Law of large numbers

If the population mean $\mu$ exists (is finite), then as the sample size grows, the sample mean approaches the population mean:

$$\text{for every } \varepsilon > 0, \qquad \Pr\!\left(\, |\hat{\mu}_N - \mu| > \varepsilon \,\right) \;\longrightarrow\; 0 \quad \text{as } N \to \infty .$$

In words: the sample mean becomes the population mean if we keep increasing the size of the sample.

Read the formula slowly. Pick any tolerance $\varepsilon$, as small as you like. The chance that the sample mean misses $\mu$ by more than $\varepsilon$ can be made as small as we like by taking $N$ large enough.

Practical examples

  • Empirical probability. In Class 1 (Section 1) we estimated a probability as the fraction of trials in which an event $E$ occurred. That fraction is the sample mean of the true/false random variable of Class 3 (Section 36), which is $1$ when $E$ occurs and $0$ otherwise, and whose population mean is $\Pr(E)$. The law of large numbers is the reason the empirical fraction approaches $\Pr(E)$.
  • Averaging repeated measurements. Averaging many readings of a quantity with statistical scatter gives a value closer and closer to the population mean.
  • Insurance and casinos. A single policy or a single bet is unpredictable, but the average over millions is very predictable. That is why an insurer can price policies, and why a casino always wins in the long run.
  • Monte Carlo simulation. A Monte Carlo code estimates a flux or a reaction rate as the average score over many simulated neutron histories. The law of large numbers guarantees that this average tends to the true value as the number of histories grows.
In real life · a criticality calculation

A Monte Carlo criticality run follows neutrons generation by generation and averages $k_{\text{eff}}$ over many generations (after discarding the first few, while the source settles). Run more generations and the average creeps towards the code's “true” answer, exactly as the dice did above.

Note what “true” means here: the exact $k_{\text{eff}}$ of the model. The law of large numbers removes statistical scatter. It does nothing about errors in the geometry or the nuclear data, which are modelling errors in the sense of Section 51.

What the law does not say

It does not correct the past

After ten Tails in a row, a fair coin is not more likely to show Head next. The tosses are independent (Class 2, Section 27).

The early excess of Tails is not cancelled; it is diluted by the many tosses that follow. After $1\,000$ more tosses we expect about $500$ Heads and $510$ Tails: the extra $10$ Tails are still there on average, but they are now only $1\%$ of the total. Believing that the coin “owes” you Heads is the gambler's fallacy.

It needs a finite mean. For the Lorentzian (Cauchy) distribution,

$$p_X(x) = \frac{1}{\pi}\,\frac{\gamma}{(x-x_0)^2 + \gamma^2},$$

the mean does not exist. Its tails fall off only like $1/x^2$, so $x\,p_X(x)$ falls off only like $1/x$, and the integral $\int x\,p_X(x)\,dx$ diverges (it grows like $\ln x$, slowly but without bound).

As a result the sample mean never settles down, however large $N$ is. One rare, huge value can always come along and drag the average far away. You saw this in Figure 8.

This shape is not merely a mathematical curiosity. It appears as the Breit–Wigner resonance in nuclear cross-sections and as the line shape in spectroscopy.

In real life · resonances

Near a resonance energy, a single isolated resonance in a neutron cross-section has the Breit–Wigner shape, which is a Lorentzian in energy (before Doppler broadening by the thermal motion of the nuclei). The large capture resonances of uranium-238, the lowest near $6.7$ eV, have this shape, and their Doppler broadening as the fuel heats up is an important safety feedback in thermal reactors.

It does not say how fast. The law tells us where $\hat\mu_N$ ends up, not how close it is for the $N$ we actually have. For that we need the central limit theorem.

Check yourself

A fair coin has shown Tails in the first $10$ tosses. (a) What is the probability of Heads on toss $11$? (b) What fraction of Heads do you expect after $1\,010$ tosses in total? (c) Does this contradict the law of large numbers?

(a) $\tfrac12$. The coin has no memory.

(b) About $500/1010 \approx 0.495$: the first ten contributed no Heads, the next thousand about $500$.

(c) No. $0.495$ is already close to $0.5$, and it gets closer as the number of tosses grows, because the fixed deficit of $10$ is divided by an ever larger total. Dilution, not correction.

Section 53The central limit theorem

The law of large numbers is about $N \to \infty$. In practice $N$ is fixed: we took $50$ counts, not infinitely many.

Imagine many laboratories, each taking a sample of the same size $N$ and computing its mean. The means would differ from lab to lab. How are these sample means distributed?

Central limit theorem

If the population has mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$, then for a fixed, large enough sample size $N$, the sample mean is approximately Gaussian:

$$\hat{\mu}_N \;\approx\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{N}\right), \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad X_1 + \dots + X_N \;\approx\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(N\mu,\ N\sigma^2\right),$$

whatever the shape of the population distribution. The approximation improves as $N$ grows.

Three separate facts are packed in here:

  1. The sample means are centred on the population mean $\mu$.
  2. Their standard deviation is $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$. This part is exact for every $N$; we will derive it in Section 58 once we have covariance.
  3. Their distribution is a Gaussian bell, even if the population is not bell-shaped at all. This is the surprising part.

Why should averaging produce a bell? Roughly: adding many independent pieces smooths out every peculiarity of the individual shape. Lumps and lopsidedness average away, and only the centre and the spread survive. The theorem says that what is left is always the same shape.

Practical examples

  • Binomial becomes Gaussian. In Class 3 (Section 46) we saw $B(N, p)$ approach the Gaussian $\mathcal{N}\big(Np,\, Np(1-p)\big)$. The number of Heads in $N$ tosses is the sum of $N$ independent true/false variables (Class 3, Section 36), each with mean $p$ and variance $p(1-p)$. The central limit theorem predicts exactly this Gaussian.
  • Poisson becomes Gaussian. A count with large mean $\lambda$ is the total of many independent small contributions (many nuclei, each rarely decaying), so it is nearly $\mathcal{N}(\lambda, \lambda)$ (Class 3, Section 46). This is what gives the $\pm\sqrt{N}$ error bar its $68\%$ meaning.
  • Why measurement noise is Gaussian. The error in a reading is often the sum of many small, independent disturbances (vibration, temperature, electronic noise, …). Whatever their individual distributions, the sum is close to Gaussian. This is why the Gaussian is the default model for random errors.
  • Dice. One die has a flat distribution. The sum of two dice is triangular (most likely total $7$). The sum of five dice already looks like a bell.

The same thing happens in the Galton board, a classic toy: each ball bounces left or right at every row of pegs, so its final bin is a sum of independent $\pm$ steps, which is binomial and therefore, for many rows, nearly Gaussian.

How large is “large enough”?

It depends on the population. For a symmetric population, $N \approx 5$ to $10$ is often enough. For a skewed one such as the exponential, $N \approx 30$ or more may be needed.

Watch the tails

The approximation is always worst far out in the tails, which matters for the tail probabilities of Chapter 12. A sample mean can look beautifully Gaussian in the middle and still have more (or fewer) extreme values than the Gaussian predicts.

Like the law of large numbers, the theorem needs a finite variance. The Lorentzian fails it: the mean of $N$ Lorentzian draws is just as spread out as a single draw.

In real life · confidence intervals for k-eff

Monte Carlo codes such as MCNP print 68%, 95% and 99% confidence intervals for $k_{\text{eff}}$. Those percentages are Gaussian probabilities: they are justified because the final estimate is an average over many generations, and the central limit theorem makes that average nearly Gaussian even though the score of a single neutron history is anything but.

Check yourself

An old trick for making Gaussian random numbers on a computer: add up $12$ independent uniform random numbers on $(0,1)$ and subtract $6$. What are the mean and standard deviation of the result, and why is it nearly Gaussian? (A uniform variable on $(0,1)$ has mean $\tfrac12$ and variance $\tfrac1{12}$.)

The sum of $12$ has mean $12 \times \tfrac12 = 6$ and variance $12 \times \tfrac1{12} = 1$ (variances of independent variables add, Section 58). Subtracting $6$ gives mean $0$ and standard deviation $1$.

It is nearly Gaussian by the central limit theorem: a sum of many independent pieces, from a symmetric population, so $12$ is plenty. (Not perfect, though: the result can never go beyond $\pm 6$, so the far tails are wrong.)

Section 54What the two laws mean for a measurement

The central limit theorem tells us how precisely a sample mean pins down $\mu$.

Standard error of the mean

The sample mean of $N$ i.i.d. measurements has standard deviation

$$\sigma_{\hat{\mu}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{N}} \;\approx\; \frac{s_N}{\sqrt{N}},$$

and we report the result as $\hat{\mu}_N \pm s_N/\sqrt{N}$. Because $\hat{\mu}_N$ is nearly Gaussian, the interval $\hat{\mu}_N \pm s_N/\sqrt{N}$ contains $\mu$ with about $68\%$ probability (Class 3, Section 46).

Worked example · the fifty counts

The fifty counts of Section 51 gave $\hat{\mu}_{50} = 7.42$ and $s_{50} = 2.76$. Report the mean count rate with its uncertainty.

Step 1: the standard error. $\sqrt{50} = 7.07$, so

$$\frac{s_{50}}{\sqrt{50}} = \frac{2.76}{7.07} = 0.39 .$$

Step 2: report.

$$7.42 \pm \frac{2.76}{\sqrt{50}} = 7.42 \pm 0.39 \text{ counts per second}.$$

The true value $7.3$ lies inside this range.

Step 3: notice the two spreads. A single one-second count scatters by $\pm 2.76$: that is the statistical uncertainty of one measurement. But the mean of $50$ such counts is uncertain only by $\pm 0.39$, seven times less.

The $\sqrt{N}$ law

Because the error bar goes like $1/\sqrt{N}$, precision is expensive:

  • to halve the uncertainty of a mean you need four times as much data;
  • to reduce it tenfold, a hundred times as much.

This is the same $1/\sqrt{N}$ rule as for counts in Class 3 (Section 46), and the reason Monte Carlo codes need $100$ times more histories for each extra decimal place of precision.

In real life · the cost of a decimal place

The relative error $R$ of a Monte Carlo tally falls like $1/\sqrt{\text{number of histories}}$, and the number of histories grows in proportion to the computer time $T$. So $R^2 T$ stays roughly constant during a well-behaved run. MCNP prints this as the figure of merit, $\mathrm{FOM} = 1/(R^2 T)$; a figure of merit that keeps drifting is a warning that the tally is not yet well sampled.

The practical lesson: a tally at $2\%$ after one hour needs about four hours for $1\%$, and a hundred hours for $0.2\%$.

In real life · opinion polls

A poll of about $1\,000$ people usually quotes a margin of error of about $\pm 3$ percentage points. For a fraction near one half, $\sigma/\sqrt{N} = \sqrt{0.25/1000} \approx 0.016$, and the quoted margin is about two of these (a $95\%$ interval). The exit poll of $2\,000$ voters in Section 49 would have about $\pm 2.2$ points. To halve the margin, a pollster needs four times as many interviews.

Chapter 12 How many sigmas?

Section 55Measuring rarity in units of $\sigma$

Thanks to the central limit theorem, many quantities in practice are close to Gaussian.

For a Gaussian, the probability of landing more than $k$ standard deviations from the mean depends only on $k$, not on $\mu$ or $\sigma$ (Class 3, Section 46). So “how many sigmas” is a universal way of saying “how rare”.

We measure a deviation in units of $\sigma$:

$$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}.$$

This $z$ is often called the z-score. A reading with $z = 2$ is two standard deviations above the mean, whatever the units of $x$.

Two kinds of tail probability are used:

  • two-sided, $\Pr(|z| > k)$: a deviation of more than $k\sigma$ in either direction;
  • one-sided, $\Pr(z > k)$: more than $k\sigma$ in one chosen direction. By symmetry it is half the two-sided value.

Worked example · is the sample above background?

A detector's background in a 100-second count is known, from a long background run, to average $400$ counts. Today a sample gives $460$ counts in 100 seconds. Is that suspicious?

Step 1: the spread. A Poisson count with mean $400$ has $\sigma = \sqrt{400} = 20$, and with such a large mean it is very nearly Gaussian (Section 53).

Step 2: how many sigmas?

$$z = \frac{460 - 400}{20} = 3 .$$

Step 3: which tail? We only care about an excess (a radioactive sample can only add counts, never remove them), so the one-sided probability is the right one: $\Pr(z > 3) = 1.35\times10^{-3}$, about 1 in $741$.

Step 4: interpret. Background alone would rarely do this, so the sample is very probably active. But be careful: a lab that measures $1\,000$ clean samples a year should expect about $1.35$ of them to show a $3\sigma$ excess by pure chance. The more places you look, the more often a rare fluctuation turns up somewhere.

In real life · five sigma and the Higgs boson

On 4 July 2012 the ATLAS and CMS experiments at CERN announced the discovery of a new particle, the Higgs boson, each with a significance of about five sigma.

Particle physicists reserve the word “discovery” for a one-sided $5\sigma$ excess: a probability of $2.9\times10^{-7}$ (about 1 in $3.5$ million) that background fluctuations alone would produce a signal at least that strong. Around $3\sigma$ they speak only of “evidence”. The bar is set so high partly because so many places are searched at once.

Sigmas only measure chance

In 2011 the OPERA experiment reported neutrinos apparently travelling faster than light, with a statistical significance of about six sigma. The cause turned out to be in the equipment, chiefly a badly connected optical-fibre cable in the timing system.

A sigma level measures only how unlikely a statistical fluctuation is. It says nothing about systematic errors (Section 51), which no amount of data averages away.

In real life · “Six Sigma” in industry

The industrial quality programme called Six Sigma quotes a target of $3.4$ defects per million, not the 1 in a billion of our table. The reason: it assumes the process mean drifts by $1.5\sigma$ over time, leaving only $4.5\sigma$ to the nearer limit, and the one-sided tail beyond $4.5\sigma$ is $3.4\times10^{-6}$.

Check yourself

A spectrum has $1\,000$ independent energy channels, all containing pure background. How many channels do you expect to deviate from their mean by more than $3\sigma$ (either way)? What does this tell you about a single $3\sigma$ bump?

Two-sided $3\sigma$: $1\,000 \times 2.7\times10^{-3} \approx 2.7$ channels, by pure chance.

So finding one $3\sigma$ bump somewhere in a big spectrum is not remarkable at all. A deviation is only as rare as the number of places you looked allows; physicists call this the look-elsewhere effect.

Section 56Joint distributions

Often one random experiment produces several numbers at once: the counts in two detectors, the length and the diameter of a pellet, the slope and the intercept of a fitted line.

Each is a random variable on the same sample space $\Omega$, and we need their probabilities together.

Discrete case

For two discrete random variables $X$ and $Y$, the joint pmf is

$$\operatorname{Pr}_{X,Y}(x, y) = \Pr(X = x \text{ and } Y = y) = \Pr\big(X^{-1}(x) \cap Y^{-1}(y)\big),$$

the probability of the intersection of two events (Class 1, Section 9 for intersections; Class 2, Section 29 for preimages).

Toss a fair coin three times. Let $X$ be the number of Heads in tosses 1 and 2, and $Y$ the number of Heads in tosses 2 and 3. Notice that toss 2 belongs to both.

Continuous case

For two continuous random variables the joint PDF $p_{X,Y}(x, y)$ is a surface over the $(x, y)$ plane.

Just as $p_X(x)\,dx$ is the probability of a thin strip (Class 3, Section 43), $p_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dx\,dy$ is the probability that $(X, Y)$ lands in a small rectangle of sides $dx$ and $dy$ at $(x, y)$. Probabilities are volumes under the surface:

Joint PDF $$\Pr\big((X, Y) \in A\big) = \iint_A p_{X,Y}(x, y)\,dx\,dy, \qquad p_{X,Y}(x,y) \ge 0,$$ $$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} p_{X,Y}(x, y)\,dx\,dy = 1 .$$

Marginal PDFs:

$$p_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} p_{X,Y}(x, y)\,dy, \qquad p_Y(y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} p_{X,Y}(x, y)\,dx .$$

Integrating out $y$ is the continuous version of summing a row of the table.

Independence

In Class 2 (Section 27), events $E$ and $E'$ are independent when $\Pr(E \cap E') = \Pr(E)\Pr(E')$. Applied to the events $\{X = x\}$ and $\{Y = y\}$, random variables are independent when the joint distribution factorises:

$$\operatorname{Pr}_{X,Y}(x, y) = \operatorname{Pr}_X(x)\,\operatorname{Pr}_Y(y) \quad\text{(discrete)}, \qquad p_{X,Y}(x, y) = p_X(x)\,p_Y(y) \quad\text{(continuous)},$$

for all $x, y$.

In the coin example they are not independent: $\operatorname{Pr}_{X,Y}(0,2) = 0$, but $\operatorname{Pr}_X(0)\operatorname{Pr}_Y(2) = \tfrac{2}{8}\cdot\tfrac{2}{8} = \tfrac1{16} \neq 0$. Both variables share toss 2.

More than two variables

Everything extends to $n$ random variables $X_1, \dots, X_n$, collected into a vector $\mathbf{X} = (X_1, \dots, X_n)$. Its joint PDF $p_{\mathbf{X}}(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ gives probabilities as $n$-fold integrals.

A random sample of Chapter 11 is an example: $n$ i.i.d. variables, whose joint PDF is the product $p_X(x_1)\,p_X(x_2)\cdots p_X(x_n)$.

In real life · neighbouring detectors

Two in-core neutron detectors in neighbouring positions see much the same local power. When the power in that region rises, both readings rise together. Describing each detector by its own distribution is not enough: only their joint distribution captures the fact that they move together, and that is what the next section measures.

Section 57Covariance and correlation

The mean and variance summarise one random variable. For two, we need one more number: do they tend to move together?

Definition

Covariance of $X$ and $Y$, with means $\mu_X$, $\mu_Y$:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \sigma_{XY} = E\big[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)\big] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (x - \mu_X)(y - \mu_Y)\, p_{X,Y}(x, y)\,dx\,dy$$

(for discrete variables, $\sum_x\sum_y (x-\mu_X)(y-\mu_Y)\operatorname{Pr}_{X,Y}(x,y)$). Expanding the product, exactly as for the variance in Class 2 (Section 34.2),

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E[XY] - \mu_X\,\mu_Y .$$

Correlation coefficient:

$$\rho_{XY} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X\,\sigma_Y}, \qquad -1 \le \rho_{XY} \le 1 .$$

The expansion takes one line. Multiply out and use $E[X] = \mu_X$, $E[Y] = \mu_Y$:

$$E\big[XY - \mu_Y X - \mu_X Y + \mu_X\mu_Y\big] = E[XY] - \mu_Y\mu_X - \mu_X\mu_Y + \mu_X\mu_Y = E[XY] - \mu_X\mu_Y .$$

Meaning of the sign

The product $(x - \mu_X)(y - \mu_Y)$ is positive when $x$ and $y$ are on the same side of their means, and negative when they are on opposite sides.

So:

  • $\operatorname{Cov} > 0$: when $X$ is above its mean, $Y$ tends to be above its mean too;
  • $\operatorname{Cov} < 0$: when $X$ is high, $Y$ tends to be low;
  • $\operatorname{Cov} = 0$: no linear tendency either way.

The covariance carries the units of $X$ times the units of $Y$. The correlation coefficient $\rho$ is dimensionless: $\rho = \pm1$ means $Y$ is an exact straight-line function of $X$.

Note also that $\operatorname{Cov}(X, X) = \sigma_X^2$: the variance is the covariance of a variable with itself.

Find $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$ and $\rho_{XY}$ for the coin example of Section 56.

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \tfrac{10}{8} - 1\cdot1 = \tfrac14 .$$

$$\rho_{XY} = \frac{1/4}{\sqrt{1/2}\,\sqrt{1/2}} = \frac{1/4}{1/2} = 0.5 .$$

Step 5: why positive? The shared toss 2: if it is a Head, both $X$ and $Y$ go up. The same thing happens in the laboratory when two measured quantities share a common ingredient, for example two spectra normalised by the same monitor count.

Independent $\Rightarrow$ uncorrelated

If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, $p_{X,Y} = p_X\,p_Y$, and the double integral splits into a product of two single integrals:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \int (x - \mu_X)\,p_X(x)\,dx \;\cdot\; \int (y - \mu_Y)\,p_Y(y)\,dy = 0 \cdot 0 = 0,$$

since the first central moment is always zero (Class 2, Section 34.1).

Uncorrelated does not imply independent

Let $X$ take the values $-1, 0, 1$ with probability $\tfrac13$ each, and let $Y = X^2$.

Then $Y$ is completely determined by $X$. Yet $E[X] = 0$ and $E[XY] = E[X^3] = \tfrac13(-1 + 0 + 1) = 0$, so $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0 - 0 \cdot E[Y] = 0$.

Key idea

Covariance detects only straight-line tendencies. Independent variables always have zero covariance, but zero covariance does not prove independence.

The covariance matrix

For $n$ random variables $X_1, \dots, X_n$ we collect all the covariances $V_{ij} = \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j)$ into an $n \times n$ matrix:

Covariance matrix $$V = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_1^2 & \operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) & \cdots & \operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_n) \\ \operatorname{Cov}(X_2, X_1) & \sigma_2^2 & \cdots & \operatorname{Cov}(X_2, X_n) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \operatorname{Cov}(X_n, X_1) & \operatorname{Cov}(X_n, X_2) & \cdots & \sigma_n^2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The diagonal holds the variances. The matrix is symmetric, $V_{ij} = V_{ji}$. For independent variables it is diagonal.

Every least-squares fitting program returns such a matrix for the fitted parameters. Its diagonal gives their squared uncertainties, and its off-diagonal entries tell how the parameters are correlated.

For reference: the multivariate Gaussian

The Gaussian of Class 3 (Section 46) extends to two variables with means $\mu_X, \mu_Y$, standard deviations $\sigma_X, \sigma_Y$ and correlation $\rho$:

$$p_{X,Y}(x, y) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_X\sigma_Y\sqrt{1-\rho^2}} \exp\!\left\{ -\frac{1}{2(1-\rho^2)}\left[ \frac{(x-\mu_X)^2}{\sigma_X^2} - \frac{2\rho(x-\mu_X)(y-\mu_Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y} + \frac{(y-\mu_Y)^2}{\sigma_Y^2} \right] \right\},$$

and to $n$ variables with mean vector $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and covariance matrix $V$:

$$p_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}\sqrt{\det V}}\exp\!\left( -\tfrac12 (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^{\mathsf T} V^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \right).$$

The surface in Figure 17 is the first formula with $\sigma_X = \sigma_Y = 1$ (and $\rho = 0.6$ at the start). A Gaussian is completely fixed by its means and its covariance matrix.

Evaluated nuclear data libraries such as ENDF/B provide not only cross-sections but, for many of them, covariance matrices. An error in a measured cross-section at one energy is usually correlated with the errors at nearby energies, because the same detector, sample and normalisation were used.

Section 58Variance of a sum

Here is the reason we needed covariance. Suppose we add (or subtract) two random quantities. How big is the spread of the result?

Variance of a sum

For any random variables $X$, $Y$ and constants $a$, $b$:

$$\operatorname{Var}(aX + bY) = a^2\sigma_X^2 + b^2\sigma_Y^2 + 2ab\,\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y).$$

If $X$ and $Y$ are independent (or merely uncorrelated), the last term vanishes and variances add.

Derivation

We only need the definition of variance and the fact that expectation is linear.

Step 1: the mean. The mean of $aX + bY$ is $a\mu_X + b\mu_Y$, so its deviation from the mean is

$$(aX + bY) - (a\mu_X + b\mu_Y) = a(X - \mu_X) + b(Y - \mu_Y).$$

Step 2: square it and take the expectation.

$$\operatorname{Var}(aX + bY) = E\Big[\big(a(X - \mu_X) + b(Y - \mu_Y)\big)^2\Big].$$

Step 3: expand the square $(A + B)^2 = A^2 + B^2 + 2AB$, term by term:

$$= a^2 E\big[(X-\mu_X)^2\big] + b^2 E\big[(Y-\mu_Y)^2\big] + 2ab\,E\big[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)\big]$$

Step 4: recognise each piece. The first is $\sigma_X^2$, the second $\sigma_Y^2$, the third $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$:

$$= a^2\sigma_X^2 + b^2\sigma_Y^2 + 2ab\,\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y). \qquad\blacksquare$$

Notice the signs. For a difference $X - Y$ we have $a = 1$, $b = -1$, so $a^2 = b^2 = 1$: the variances still add, while the covariance term flips sign. See it happen:

The standard error of the mean, at last

For an i.i.d. sample, all covariances between different $X_i$ are zero. The formula above extends to any number of terms, so the variance of the sum is just the sum of the variances:

$$\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac{X_1 + \dots + X_N}{N}\right) = \frac{1}{N^2}\big(\sigma^2 + \sigma^2 + \dots + \sigma^2\big) = \frac{N\sigma^2}{N^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{N},$$

which is the $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$ promised in Section 53. (The $1/N^2$ comes from $a^2$ with $a = 1/N$.)

Independence was essential. Correlated readings, for example all affected by the same slow temperature drift, average down more slowly.

A closer look · how much more slowly?

Model each reading as $X_i = \mu + D + e_i$: an offset $D$ shared by all $N$ readings (a drift, a calibration error) with variance $\rho\sigma^2$, plus independent noise $e_i$ with variance $(1-\rho)\sigma^2$. Each reading still has variance $\sigma^2$, and any two readings have covariance $\operatorname{Var}(D) = \rho\sigma^2$.

The mean is $\hat\mu_N = \mu + D + \bar e$. The shared $D$ does not average at all; only the noise does:

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu_N) = \rho\sigma^2 + \frac{(1-\rho)\sigma^2}{N} = \frac{\sigma^2}{N}\big[1 + (N-1)\rho\big].$$

As $N \to \infty$ this does not go to zero but to $\rho\sigma^2$: a floor of $\sigma\sqrt{\rho}$ that no amount of averaging can beat.

Check with the coins

In the three-toss example, $X + Y$ has variance

$$\operatorname{Var}(X+Y) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 + 2\cdot\tfrac14 = \tfrac32 .$$

Directly: $X + Y = T_1 + 2T_2 + T_3$, where $T_i$ is $1$ for a Head on toss $i$ (variance $\tfrac14$ each, all independent). Toss 2 is counted twice, so its coefficient is $2$ and its variance gets multiplied by $2^2 = 4$:

$$\operatorname{Var}(X+Y) = \tfrac14 + 4\cdot\tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac32 . \quad\checkmark$$

A slow drift, or an offset shared by every sample, is perfectly correlated from one sample to the next, so it survives any amount of averaging. It has to be found by calibration instead.

Section 59Propagation of uncertainty

We rarely report a raw measurement. We compute something from it: a count rate from a count and a time, a momentum transfer from an angle and a wavelength. If the inputs are uncertain, how uncertain is the result?

The idea in one sentence: if an input wobbles a little, the result wobbles too, and the slope of the formula with respect to that input sets how much. The partial derivatives act like gear ratios.

Let $f(X, Y)$ be computed from two uncertain inputs. If the uncertainties are small, $f$ is nearly linear over the range where $X$ and $Y$ scatter. So we may use the first-order Taylor expansion about the means:

$$f(X, Y) \approx f(\mu_X, \mu_Y) + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(X - \mu_X) + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(Y - \mu_Y),$$

with the derivatives evaluated at $(\mu_X, \mu_Y)$. This is of the form $\text{constant} + aX + bY$, with $a = \partial f/\partial x$ and $b = \partial f/\partial y$. A constant does not change a variance, so Section 58 gives the answer at once:

Propagation of uncertainty (first order) $$\sigma_f^2 = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)^{\!2}\sigma_X^2 + \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)^{\!2}\sigma_Y^2 + 2\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y).$$

For $n$ inputs:

$$\sigma_f^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}\,V_{ij}.$$

This is the “law of propagation of uncertainty” of the international Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement (GUM). The familiar “add in quadrature” rule is the special case of independent inputs, where the covariance term is dropped.

Example 1 · background subtraction (independent inputs)

A peak window holds $G = 1600$ gross counts; a background run gives $B = 900$ counts. Find the net signal $S = G - B$ and its uncertainty.

Step 1: the value. $S = 1600 - 900 = 700$.

Step 2: the input variances. The two counts are independent Poisson variables, so $\sigma_G^2 = 1600$ and $\sigma_B^2 = 900$.

Step 3: the derivatives. $\partial S/\partial G = 1$ and $\partial S/\partial B = -1$; their squares are both $1$.

Step 4: combine.

$$\sigma_S^2 = 1600 + 900 = 2500, \qquad S = 700 \pm 50 .$$

Step 5: notice. The uncertainties add even though the counts are subtracted. Each count alone is uncertain by about $2.5$–$3\%$ ($40/1600$ and $30/900$), but the net signal by $50/700 = 7\%$. Subtracting a large background is expensive.

Example 2 · products and ratios (independent inputs)

Show that for $f = XY$ or $f = X/Y$ with independent inputs, relative uncertainties add in quadrature.

Step 1: the product. For $f = XY$: $\partial f/\partial x = Y$ and $\partial f/\partial y = X$, so

$$\sigma_f^2 = Y^2\sigma_X^2 + X^2\sigma_Y^2 .$$

Step 2: divide by $f^2 = X^2Y^2$.

$$\left(\frac{\sigma_f}{f}\right)^{\!2} = \left(\frac{\sigma_X}{X}\right)^{\!2} + \left(\frac{\sigma_Y}{Y}\right)^{\!2}.$$

Step 3: the ratio. For $f = X/Y$: $\partial f/\partial x = 1/Y$ and $\partial f/\partial y = -X/Y^2$, so $\sigma_f^2 = \sigma_X^2/Y^2 + X^2\sigma_Y^2/Y^4$. Dividing by $f^2 = X^2/Y^2$ gives exactly the same formula: relative uncertainties add in quadrature.

Step 4: a count rate. For $R = N/t$ with a precise timer ($\sigma_t \approx 0$), $\sigma_R/R = \sigma_N/N = \sqrt{N}/N = 1/\sqrt{N}$.

Example 3 · a neutron diffraction peak

The momentum transfer is $q = 4\pi\sin\theta/\lambda$. With $\lambda = 4.00 \pm 0.04$ Å and $\theta = 30.0^\circ \pm 0.1^\circ$ (independent), find $q$ and $\sigma_q$.

Step 1: the value. $\sin 30^\circ = 0.5$, so

$$q = \frac{4\pi \times 0.5}{4.00} = 1.571 \text{ Å}^{-1}.$$

Step 2: the derivatives.

$$\frac{\partial q}{\partial \lambda} = -\frac{4\pi\sin\theta}{\lambda^2} = -\frac{q}{\lambda}, \qquad \frac{\partial q}{\partial \theta} = \frac{4\pi\cos\theta}{\lambda} = q\cot\theta .$$

Step 3: angles in radians! The derivative with respect to $\theta$ assumes radians, so $\sigma_\theta = 0.1^\circ = 0.1 \times \pi/180 = 1.745\times10^{-3}$ rad.

Step 4: relative uncertainty. Dividing the propagation formula by $q^2$:

$$\frac{\sigma_q}{q} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{0.04}{4.00}\right)^2 + \left(\cot 30^\circ \times 1.745\times10^{-3}\right)^2} = \sqrt{(0.0100)^2 + (0.0030)^2} = 0.0104 .$$

Step 5: result. $\sigma_q = 0.0104 \times 1.571 = 0.016$, so $q = 1.571 \pm 0.016$ Å$^{-1}$.

The wavelength spread dominates. Because the terms add in quadrature, the small one hardly matters: improving the angle measurement would barely help.

Example 4 · why the covariance term matters

A straight-line calibration $y = a + bx$ is fitted to data. The fit returns $a = 2.0 \pm 0.3$, $b = 0.50 \pm 0.05$ and correlation $\rho_{ab} = -0.9$. Find the calibrated value at $x_0 = 10$ and its uncertainty.

Step 1: the covariance.

$$\operatorname{Cov}(a, b) = \rho_{ab}\,\sigma_a\,\sigma_b = -0.9 \times 0.3 \times 0.05 = -0.0135 .$$

(Intercept and slope of a fit are typically strongly anti-correlated: if the line is tilted a little steeper, it must start a little lower to pass through the data.)

Step 2: the value and the derivatives. $y_0 = a + 10\,b = 2.0 + 5.0 = 7.0$, with $\partial y_0/\partial a = 1$ and $\partial y_0/\partial b = 10$.

Step 3: propagate, covariance included.

$$\sigma_{y_0}^2 = 0.3^2 + 10^2 \times 0.05^2 + 2 \times 1 \times 10 \times (-0.0135) = 0.09 + 0.25 - 0.27 = 0.07,$$

so $y_0 = 7.0 \pm 0.26$.

Step 4: what if we forgot it? Ignoring the covariance would give $\sqrt{0.09 + 0.25} = \sqrt{0.34} = 0.58$, more than twice too large.

With a positive correlation, ignoring it would instead underestimate the uncertainty, which is the more dangerous mistake.

When is first order enough?

The linear approximation needs $f$ to be nearly straight over a range of a few $\sigma$ around the means.

For strongly non-linear functions or large uncertainties, one instead simulates: draw many random inputs from their joint distribution, compute $f$ for each, and look at the spread of the results. This is a Monte Carlo propagation, again justified by the law of large numbers. The animation below does exactly that and compares it with the first-order formula.

In real life · reactivity from k-eff

Reactivity is computed from the multiplication factor as $\rho_r = (k-1)/k = 1 - 1/k$ (here $\rho_r$ is reactivity, not a correlation). Its derivative is $d\rho_r/dk = 1/k^2$, so $\sigma_{\rho_r} = \sigma_k/k^2$.

For a Monte Carlo result $k = 1.00200 \pm 0.00030$ this gives $\rho_r \approx 200$ pcm with $\sigma_{\rho_r} \approx 30$ pcm. A control-rod worth found as the difference of two independent runs, each $\pm 30$ pcm, is uncertain by $\sqrt{30^2 + 30^2} \approx 42$ pcm, just like the background subtraction in Example 1. That is why small worths need long runs or special techniques that make the two calculations positively correlated.

Common pitfall

Dropping the covariance term is only safe when the inputs really are independent. If two inputs share an ingredient (the same calibration, the same monitor count, the same nuclear data) and enter the result with derivatives of the same sign, the true uncertainty is larger than “add in quadrature” says.

Check yourself

A ratio $R = A/B$ with independent $A = 400 \pm 20$ and $B = 100 \pm 10$. Find $R$ and $\sigma_R$.

$R = 4.0$. The relative uncertainties are $5\%$ and $10\%$, so

$$\frac{\sigma_R}{R} = \sqrt{0.05^2 + 0.10^2} = \sqrt{0.0125} = 0.112,$$

and $R = 4.0 \pm 0.45$. The larger relative error dominates.

Section 60The sandwich rule

Often we compute several results from the same inputs. Then we also need the covariances between the results. Matrix notation handles all of this in one line.

Let the inputs be the vector $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ with covariance matrix $V_x$ ($n\times n$), and the results be $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \dots, y_m)$ with $y_k = f_k(\mathbf{x})$.

Collect the derivatives into the $m \times n$ Jacobian matrix $J$, with $J_{ki} = \partial f_k/\partial x_i$. Row $k$ is the gradient of $f_k$. Then:

Sandwich rule $$V_y = J\, V_x\, J^{\mathsf T} .$$

$V_y$ is the $m \times m$ covariance matrix of the results: its diagonal holds the variances of the results, its off-diagonal entries their covariances.

The name comes from the shape: the input covariance $V_x$ is the filling, sandwiched between two slices of Jacobian, $J$ and its transpose $J^{\mathsf T}$.

It contains everything we did before

With one result ($m = 1$) and two inputs, $J = \begin{pmatrix} \partial f/\partial x & \partial f/\partial y \end{pmatrix}$ is a single row. Writing $f_x = \partial f/\partial x$ and $f_y = \partial f/\partial y$:

$$J V_x J^{\mathsf T} = \begin{pmatrix} f_x & f_y \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_X^2 & \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) \\ \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) & \sigma_Y^2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} f_x \\ f_y \end{pmatrix} = f_x^2\sigma_X^2 + 2 f_x f_y \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) + f_y^2 \sigma_Y^2 .$$

This is exactly the propagation formula of Section 59. For Example 4 there, $J = (1 \;\; 10)$ and

$$\sigma_{y_0}^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 10 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0.09 & -0.0135 \\ -0.0135 & 0.0025 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 10 \end{pmatrix} = 0.07 .$$

(Multiply it out: the row times the matrix is $(0.09 - 0.135,\ -0.0135 + 0.025) = (-0.045,\ 0.0115)$, and that times the column is $-0.045 + 0.115 = 0.07$.)

Worked example · independent inputs can give correlated results

Two detectors record independent Poisson counts $A = 900$ and $B = 400$. We report the total $S = A + B$ and the difference $D = A - B$. Find the covariance matrix of $(S, D)$.

Step 1: the input covariance. Independent Poisson counts, so the variances equal the counts and the off-diagonal is zero:

$$V_x = \begin{pmatrix} 900 & 0 \\ 0 & 400 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Step 2: the Jacobian. Row 1 is the gradient of $S$, row 2 that of $D$:

$$J = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Step 3: the first multiplication.

$$J V_x = \begin{pmatrix} 900 & 400 \\ 900 & -400 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Step 4: the second multiplication.

$$V_y = J V_x J^{\mathsf T} = \begin{pmatrix} 900 & 400 \\ 900 & -400 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1300 & 500 \\ 500 & 1300 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Step 5: read it off. $S = 1300 \pm 36$ and $D = 500 \pm 36$ (since $\sqrt{1300} = 36$). But $S$ and $D$ are correlated:

$$\rho_{SD} = \frac{500}{\sqrt{1300}\sqrt{1300}} = \frac{500}{1300} \approx 0.38 .$$

An upward fluctuation of $A$ raises both $S$ and $D$.

Anyone who later combines $S$ and $D$ must use this covariance, not just the two error bars. This is why careful experiments publish the full covariance matrix of their results, not only the uncertainties.

In real life · three estimates of k-eff

MCNP estimates $k_{\text{eff}}$ in three different ways (collision, absorption and track-length estimators) from the same simulated neutrons. Because they use the same histories, the three estimates are strongly correlated. To combine them into one best value with an honest uncertainty, the code uses their covariance matrix rather than treating them as three independent measurements.

Check yourself

In the detector example, what happens to $\rho_{SD}$ if the two counts have equal variances, $\sigma_A = \sigma_B$? Use the sandwich rule with $V_x = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_A^2, \sigma_B^2)$.

The same multiplication as in the worked example gives

$$V_y = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2 & \sigma_A^2 - \sigma_B^2 \\ \sigma_A^2 - \sigma_B^2 & \sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

With $\sigma_A = \sigma_B$ the off-diagonal entries vanish, so $\rho_{SD} = 0$: sum and difference are then uncorrelated. (You can check this in Figure 27 by setting the two counts equal.)

Five sentences to remember

  • A sample is a random draw from a population; its statistics jitter, its parameters do not.
  • Even a perfect experiment scatters when the phenomenon is random, and that scatter shrinks only by collecting more data.
  • Averages converge (law of large numbers), are nearly Gaussian, and have error bars $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$ (central limit theorem), as long as the mean and variance exist.
  • “How many sigmas” turns a deviation into a probability, but only for statistical fluctuations of a Gaussian quantity.
  • When quantities share ingredients they are correlated, and the covariance must travel with them: $\operatorname{Var}(aX+bY)$, the propagation formula and $V_y = JV_xJ^{\mathsf T}$ all carry it.
